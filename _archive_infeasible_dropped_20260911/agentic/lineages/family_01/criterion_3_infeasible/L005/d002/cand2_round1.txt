<STATEMENT>
# Problem C — Justifying the Roster Failure

Time limit: 2 s
Memory limit: 256 MB

The management wants to assign all personnel to two distinct working periods. They have collected a list of preliminary friction reports, each indicating that two specific individuals should ideally not share a working period. 

However, management will only formally declare the two-period assignment impossible if they can identify a *roster failure*. A roster failure is defined as a set of exactly three distinct individuals such that, if the entire list of preliminary reports is filtered to keep only the reports where both involved individuals belong to this specific set of three, exactly three reports will be kept.

When such a situation occurs, it is strictly unachievable to divide these three individuals into just two periods without someone sharing a period with someone they have a report against.

The management guarantees that the provided records contain at least one such set of three individuals. Your task is to identify one such set to justify the roster failure.

## Input
The first line contains two integers $n$ and $m$ ($3 \le n \le 2000$, $3 \le m \le \min(n(n-1)/2, 2 \cdot 10^5)$), representing the number of personnel and the number of preliminary reports, respectively. Personnel are numbered from 1 to $n$.

Each of the next $m$ lines contains two integers $u$ and $v$ ($1 \le u, v \le n$, $u \ne v$), indicating a preliminary report involving individual $u$ and individual $v$. All reports are distinct (no pair of individuals is listed more than once).

It is guaranteed that at least one roster failure (and thus its associated set of three individuals) exists.

## Output
Print three integers $a$, $b$, and $c$ on a single line, satisfying $a < b < c$, representing the three individuals involved in the roster failure.

If there are multiple such sets, output the one that comes first lexicographically (minimize $a$; in case of a tie, minimize $b$; and finally minimize $c$).

## Example 1
Input:
```
4 5
1 2
1 3
2 3
2 4
3 4
```
Output:
```
1 2 3
```

## Example 2
Input:
```
5 6
1 2
1 4
2 4
3 4
3 5
4 5
```
Output:
```
1 2 4
```
</STATEMENT>
<RATIONALE>
Changed the definition of the target structure from a pair of individuals sharing a corroborator to a set of three individuals that exclusively contains exactly three reports. This forces the model to deduce that three distinct reports among three individuals implies all pairwise connections (a triangle) rather than simply pattern-matching the "common neighbor" wording.
</RATIONALE>
<PRESERVED_ANSWER>
Because all reports are distinct, a set of three individuals can have at most three reports between them; having exactly three means every pair in the set is connected, which is precisely a triangle.
</PRESERVED_ANSWER>
<PRESERVED_CLASS>
The underlying problem remains finding a triangle (a 3-cycle) in an undirected graph, which is solvable in O(nm/64) using bitsets.
</PRESERVED_CLASS>
