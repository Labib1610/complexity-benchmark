Because the graph is simple, three edges forming a subgraph with only even degrees must be a triangle. <STATEMENT>
# Problem C — Justifying the Roster Failure

Time limit: 2 s
Memory limit: 256 MB

The management wants to assign all personnel to two distinct working periods. They have collected a list of incompatibility logs, each indicating that two specific individuals are in conflict and cannot share a working period. 

However, management will only formally declare the two-period assignment impossible if they can pinpoint a specific failing configuration. They require a proof of impossibility consisting of exactly three distinct incompatibility logs chosen from the provided records. 

A set of exactly three logs is considered a valid proof if it satisfies the following parity condition: when you tally the occurrences of each individual across the three chosen logs, every individual who appears must have a tally that is an even number. Since each log involves two people, this means no individual is left with an odd number of appearances across the three logs.

The management guarantees that the provided records contain at least one such set of three logs. Your task is to identify the three unique individuals involved in this proof.

## Input
The first line contains two integers $n$ and $m$ ($3 \le n \le 2000$, $3 \le m \le \min(n(n-1)/2, 2 \cdot 10^5)$), representing the number of personnel and the total number of incompatibility logs, respectively. Personnel are numbered from 1 to $n$.

Each of the next $m$ lines contains two integers $u$ and $v$ ($1 \le u, v \le n$, $u \ne v$), indicating an incompatibility log involving individual $u$ and individual $v$. All logs are distinct (no pair of individuals is listed more than once).

It is guaranteed that at least one valid set of three logs exists.

## Output
Print three integers $a$, $b$, and $c$ on a single line, satisfying $a < b < c$, representing the three unique individuals involved in the valid set of logs.

If there are multiple valid groups of three individuals that can be formed this way, output the one that comes first lexicographically (minimize $a$; in case of a tie, minimize $b$; and finally minimize $c$).

## Example 1
Input:
```
4 5
1 2
1 3
2 3
2 4
3 4
```
Output:
```
1 2 3
```

## Example 2
Input:
```
5 6
1 2
1 4
2 4
3 4
3 5
4 5
```
Output:
```
1 2 4
```
</STATEMENT>
<RATIONALE>
The definition of the failing configuration was changed from "three logs mentioning exactly three unique individuals" to "three logs where every mentioned individual appears an even number of times." Because the logs restrict the graph to be simple (no self-loops or parallel edges), any subset of three edges where all vertex degrees are even must be a triangle (3 vertices of degree 2). This forces the model to deduce that the parity condition is mathematically equivalent to finding a triangle, preventing it from relying on surface-level keyword recognition.
</RATIONALE>
<PRESERVED_ANSWER>
The parity condition for exactly three distinct edges in a simple graph is uniquely satisfied by a triangle (where three individuals each appear exactly twice), which is mathematically identical to the original requirement of three edges spanning exactly three individuals.
</PRESERVED_ANSWER>
<PRESERVED_CLASS>
The problem remains exactly the detection of a triangle in an undirected graph, keeping it in the same complexity class with the identical optimal O(nm/64) bitset approach.
</PRESERVED_CLASS>
<RISK>
Models might misinterpret "tally the occurrences" if they fail to realize that an individual's appearance in a log counts as one occurrence, but the statement clarifies this by stating "each log involves two people, this means no individual is left with an odd number of appearances."
</RISK>
