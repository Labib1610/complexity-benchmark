<STATEMENT>
# Problem C — Justifying the Roster Failure

Time limit: 2 s
Memory limit: 256 MB

The management wants to assign all personnel to two distinct working periods. They have collected a list of incompatibility logs, each indicating that two specific individuals are in conflict and cannot share a working period. 

However, management will only formally declare the two-period assignment impossible if they can pinpoint a specific failing configuration. They require a proof of impossibility consisting of exactly three distinct incompatibility logs chosen from the provided records. 

This selection of three logs is considered a valid proof if the total number of unique personnel IDs mentioned across the three chosen logs is exactly three.

The management guarantees that the provided records contain at least one such selection of logs. Your task is to identify the three individuals involved in this proof.

## Input
The first line contains two integers $n$ and $m$, representing the number of personnel and the total number of incompatibility logs, respectively. Personnel are numbered from 1 to $n$. 

The minimum value for both $n$ and $m$ is 3. The maximum possible number of personnel $n$ is equal to the number of kilograms in exactly two metric tonnes. The maximum possible number of logs $m$ is limited by the total number of possible pairs of personnel, or the number of centimeters in exactly two kilometers, whichever is smaller.

Each of the next $m$ lines contains two integers $u$ and $v$ ($1 \le u, v \le n$, $u \ne v$), indicating an incompatibility log involving individual $u$ and individual $v$. All logs are distinct (no pair of individuals is listed more than once).

It is guaranteed that at least one valid selection of three logs exists.

## Output
Print three integers $a$, $b$, and $c$ on a single line, satisfying $a < b < c$, representing the individuals involved in the valid selection of logs.

If there are multiple valid groups of individuals that can be formed this way, output the one that comes first lexicographically (minimize $a$; in case of a tie, minimize $b$; and finally minimize $c$).

## Example 1
Input:
```
4 5
1 2
1 3
2 3
2 4
3 4
```
Output:
```
1 2 3
```

## Example 2
Input:
```
5 6
1 2
1 4
2 4
3 4
3 5
4 5
```
Output:
```
1 2 4
```

<METHOD_NAME>Vertex-Edge Count Equivalency</METHOD_NAME>
<METHOD_DESCRIPTION>Replaces the parity/even-degree condition (which models easily map to a cycle/triangle) with a structurally equivalent condition based on the total number of unique entities across a fixed number of sets. Specifically, 3 simple edges involving exactly 3 unique vertices must form a triangle. This avoids all graph-theoretic and parity vocabulary, forcing the model to deduce the structure from the vertex-to-edge ratio.</METHOD_DESCRIPTION>
<METHOD_NOVEL>yes</METHOD_NOVEL>
<RATIONALE>
Removed the parity condition ("appear an even number of times") which models heavily relied on to recognize cycles/triangles. Replaced it with the mathematically equivalent condition that 3 distinct edges on a simple graph involving exactly 3 unique vertices must form a triangle. Also applied indirect physical bounds for $n$ and $m$ to force derivation of limits.
</RATIONALE>
<PRESERVED_ANSWER>
Three distinct edges (logs) that collectively involve exactly three unique vertices can only be a triangle, which is exactly the same structural requirement as the original parity condition.
</PRESERVED_ANSWER>
<PRESERVED_CLASS>
The problem still requires finding a triangle (or 3-clique) in an unweighted undirected graph, keeping the time complexity identically $O(m \sqrt{m})$ or $O(nm/64)$.
</PRESERVED_CLASS>
<RISK>
Models might struggle to parse the physical unit conversions for the bounds (kilograms in two metric tonnes, centimeters in two kilometers), though they are standard metric conversions.
</RISK>
