# Problem C — Proof of Impossibility

Time limit: 2 s   Memory limit: 256 MB

The laboratory still has only two storage rooms, 1 and 2, but the inventory segregation is known to be hopeless. Your job is not to assign rooms — it is to certify the impossibility. It is guaranteed that the reaction logs contain at least one group of three chemicals where two of the chemicals react with each other, and the remaining chemical reacts with both of those two. Any such group proves two rooms cannot suffice. Output one such group.

## Input
The first line contains two integers n and m (3 ≤ n ≤ 2000,
3 ≤ m ≤ min(n·(n−1)/2, 2·10⁵)).
Each of the next m lines contains two integers u v — a reacting pair of chemicals.
It is guaranteed that at least one such group exists.

## Output
Print three integers a b c on one line with a < b < c such that among the three chemicals, two of them react with each other, and the remaining one reacts with both of those two. Output the lexicographically smallest such sequence (smallest a; ties by smallest b; then smallest c).

## Example 1
Input:
```
4 5
1 2
1 3
2 3
2 4
3 4
```
Output:
```
1 2 3
```

## Example 2
Input:
```
5 6
1 2
1 4
2 4
3 4
3 5
4 5
```
Output:
```
1 2 4
```
</STATEMENT>
<RATIONALE>
Changed the description of the required output from a set that "pairwise reacts" and explicitly listing "a-b, a-c, b-c" to a structural description: two chemicals that react, and a remaining chemical that reacts with both. This forces models to logically deduce that all three pairs must react (a triangle) rather than relying on the explicit "pairwise" keyword or the explicit list of all three edges to pattern-match the graph concept.
</RATIONALE>
<PRESERVED_ANSWER>
The structural condition (two react, and the remaining one reacts with both) logically requires that all three possible pairs among the three chemicals must react, which is mathematically identical to the seed problem's requirement.
</PRESERVED_ANSWER>
