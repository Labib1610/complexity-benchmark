# Triple-Point Interference

Time limit: 2 s
Memory limit: 256 MB

An experimental communication network consists of $n$ transceivers. During testing, the engineers recorded $m$ instances of bidirectional interference. Each instance indicates that two specific transceivers disrupt each other's signals and cannot operate on the same frequency. 

The team needs to demonstrate the severity of the interference by finding a completely jammed triad. A jammed triad is a group of exactly three transceivers where every possible pair within the group experiences bidirectional interference. Finding such a triad by checking all possible combinations of three transceivers can be slow, but it is necessary for the final diagnostic report.

You must identify one jammed triad of transceivers. The test logs guarantee that at least one such triad exists. Output the three transceiver IDs in increasing order. To ensure standardized reporting, if multiple triads exist, output the one that is lexicographically first (i.e., the smallest first ID; in case of a tie, the smallest second ID; and finally the smallest third ID).

## Input
The first line contains two integers $n$ and $m$ ($3 \le n \le 2000$, $3 \le m \le \min(n \cdot (n-1)/2, 2\cdot 10^5)$), representing the number of transceivers and the number of interference records.
Each of the next $m$ lines contains two integers $u$ and $v$ ($1 \le u, v \le n$, $u \neq v$) — a pair of transceivers that interfere with each other. Each interfering pair is listed at most once.
It is guaranteed that at least one jammed triad of exactly three transceivers exists.

## Output
Print three integers $a$, $b$, and $c$ on a single line with $a < b < c$ such that all three pairs ($a$ and $b$, $a$ and $c$, $b$ and $c$) appear in the interference records. Output the lexicographically smallest such triplet.

## Example 1
Input:
```
4 5
1 2
1 3
2 3
2 4
3 4
```
Output:
```
1 2 3
```

## Example 2
Input:
```
5 6
1 2
1 4
2 4
3 4
3 5
4 5
```
Output:
```
1 2 4
```
