<STATEMENT>
# Problem C — Proof of Collusion

Time limit: 2 s
Memory limit: 256 MB

An auditor is analyzing a set of transaction logs among regional suppliers. Each log entry involves a pair of suppliers. For any such logged pair, a "forward audit trail" is established originating from the supplier with the smaller identifier and terminating at the supplier with the larger identifier.

You already know that dividing these suppliers into two independent oversight tiers is hopeless. Your task is to provide a concrete proof of this impossibility by identifying a trio of suppliers who are completely deadlocked.

A deadlock occurs when a two-step forward audit sequence is completely redundant. Specifically, a two-step sequence consists of a starting supplier, an intermediate supplier, and a final supplier, joined by a forward audit trail from the start to the intermediate, and another from the intermediate to the final. This sequence is *bypassed* (creating the deadlock) if there is also a direct forward audit trail from the starting supplier to the final supplier.

Identify the starting, intermediate, and final suppliers forming such a bypassed sequence.

## Input
The first line contains two integers: the total number of regional suppliers, and the number of transaction logs. The total number of suppliers is at least 3 and at most 2000. The suppliers are numbered consecutively starting from 1. The number of transaction logs is at least 3, at most 200000, and will not exceed the maximum possible number of strictly distinct pairs among the suppliers.

Each of the following lines contains two integers representing a pair of suppliers involved in a transaction log entry.

It is guaranteed that the records contain at least one trio of suppliers satisfying the deadlock condition.

## Output
Print three integers on one line: the starting, intermediate, and final suppliers of the bypassed sequence. If multiple such deadlocked trios exist, output the combination that is lexicographically smallest (minimize the starting supplier's identifier, then the intermediate's, then the final's).

## Example 1
Input:
```
4 5
1 2
1 3
2 3
2 4
3 4
```
Output:
```
1 2 3
```

## Example 2
Input:
```
5 6
1 2
1 4
2 4
3 4
3 5
4 5
```
Output:
```
1 2 4
```

<RATIONALE>
The problem was returned to the seed's exact domain (auditors, regional suppliers, oversight tiers) to ensure strict adherence to the original task. The explicit `a < b < c` notation and the three distinct edge conditions were removed. Instead, the problem defines a "bypassed two-step sequence", forcing the model to deduce the implicit ordering and the triangle structure from the definition of a forward audit trail. The bounds were restated indirectly to satisfy Strategy S07.
</RATIONALE>
<PRESERVED_ANSWER>
The definition of a forward audit trail enforces that the starting supplier is less than the intermediate, and the intermediate is less than the final; thus, finding a bypassed sequence is exactly equivalent to finding $a < b < c$ with edges $(a,b), (b,c), (a,c)$.
</PRESERVED_ANSWER>
<PRESERVED_CLASS>
The core computational task remains finding a 3-clique (triangle) in the graph, preserving the infeasibility/certificate nature of the problem.
</PRESERVED_CLASS>
<RISK>
Models might misinterpret "bypassed two-step sequence" if they fail to link the directionality of the "forward audit trail" to the strict numeric ordering of the suppliers, but the definition is mathematically unambiguous.
</RISK>
