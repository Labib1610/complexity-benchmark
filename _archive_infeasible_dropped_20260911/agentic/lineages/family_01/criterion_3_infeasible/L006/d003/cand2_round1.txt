# Problem C — Proof of Reactivity

Time limit: 2 s
Memory limit: 256 MB

A geneticist is attempting to separate $n$ protein sequences (identified by integers $1$ through $n$) into exactly two non-reactive storage vessels. However, certain pairs of sequences exhibit mutual binding affinity. Two sequences with such an affinity cannot be placed in the same vessel, as they would react and degrade.

The geneticist already knows that a two-vessel separation is hopeless. Your task is not to attempt the separation, but to provide concrete proof of its impossibility. You must identify three distinct sequences. Your evidence must show that the first binds with the second, and additionally, that the third binds with both the first and the second. Because these three sequences are all mutually reactive, they cannot be separated into only two vessels without at least two of them ending up in the same vessel.

## Input
The first line contains two integers: the sequence headcount $n$ and the affinity count $m$. The headcount $n$ ranges from the minimum required for the proof (three) up to the physical tray capacity (two thousand). The affinity count $m$ is at least three, and at most either the maximum possible number of unique pairs among the $n$ sequences, or the database's hard limit of two hundred thousand (whichever is smaller).
Each of the next $m$ lines contains two integers $u$ and $v$ — a pair of sequences that exhibit mutual binding affinity.
It is guaranteed that at least one valid configuration of three sequences exists.

## Output
Print three integers on one line, representing the identifiers of the three sequences in increasing numerical order. If there are multiple valid sets of three sequences, output the lexicographically smallest combination (the one with the smallest first identifier; ties broken by the smallest second identifier; then the smallest third identifier).

## Example 1
Input:
```
4 5
1 2
1 3
2 3
2 4
3 4
```
Output:
```
1 2 3
```

## Example 2
Input:
```
5 6
1 2
1 4
2 4
3 4
3 5
4 5
```
Output:
```
1 2 4
```

<RATIONALE>
The explicit definition of "every pair among them is bound" was replaced with an asymmetric description ("the first binds with the second, and additionally, that the third binds with both"). This breaks simple pattern matching for "clique" or "triangle" by framing the structure as an edge and a common neighbor, forcing the model to deduce that this still forms a fully connected triplet. The numeric bounds were also rewritten into prose scenario limits (S07).
</RATIONALE>
<PRESERVED_ANSWER>
The required output is still the lexicographically smallest set of three mutually connected nodes, so the answer for any given graph remains identical.
</PRESERVED_ANSWER>
<PRESERVED_CLASS>
The problem still asks for the detection of a triangle (3-clique) in an undirected graph, keeping it solvable in O(n*m/64) using bitset intersections.
</PRESERVED_CLASS>
