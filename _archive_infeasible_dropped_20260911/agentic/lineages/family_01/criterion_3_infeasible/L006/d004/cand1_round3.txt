<STATEMENT>
# Problem C — Proof of Collusion

Time limit: 2 s
Memory limit: 256 MB

An auditor is attempting to divide $n$ regional suppliers (identified by integers $1$ through $n$) into exactly two independent oversight tiers. However, the records contain $m$ bilateral non-compete agreements, each between a specific pair of suppliers. Two suppliers sharing such an agreement cannot be placed in the same oversight tier, as they would collude to manipulate the audit.

The auditor already knows that a two-tier division is hopeless for the given records. Your task is not to attempt the division, but to provide a concrete proof of its impossibility. To do this, you must find three distinct suppliers $a$, $b$, and $c$. The proof requires that all three pairs $(a,b)$, $(b,c)$, and $(a,c)$ appear as bilateral agreements in the input records. This mutual incompatibility creates an unresolvable deadlock, preventing the three suppliers from being split across just two tiers.

Output the identifiers of these three suppliers. It is guaranteed that the records contain at least one such valid proof.

## Input
The first line contains two integers $n$ and $m$ ($3 \le n \le 2000$, $3 \le m \le \min(n \cdot (n-1)/2, 2 \cdot 10^5)$).
Each of the next $m$ lines contains two integers $u$ and $v$ — a pair of suppliers who share a bilateral agreement. (Each pair appears at most once in the records).
It is guaranteed that at least one combination of three suppliers satisfying the proof condition exists.

## Output
Print three integers $a$, $b$, and $c$ on one line with $a < b < c$, representing the identifiers of the three suppliers. Output the lexicographically smallest such combination (smallest $a$; ties broken by smallest $b$; then smallest $c$).

## Example 1
Input:
```
4 5
1 2
1 3
2 3
2 4
3 4
```
Output:
```
1 2 3
```

## Example 2
Input:
```
5 6
1 2
1 4
2 4
3 4
3 5
4 5
```
Output:
```
1 2 4
```
</STATEMENT>
<RATIONALE>
The seed problem's phrasing "count... the total is exactly three" was acting as a strong trigger for models to recognize a triangle/3-clique. By replacing this with a direct edge-set requirement that pairs (a,b), (b,c), and (a,c) must exist, models are forced to parse the mathematical structure without relying on keyword shortcuts like "trio" or "exactly three", while strictly adhering to the exact mathematical condition to pass G3.
</RATIONALE>
<PRESERVED_ANSWER>
The condition that pairs (a,b), (b,c), and (a,c) exist is mathematically identical to finding three nodes with three edges between them, meaning the same lexicographically smallest triangle is always found.
</PRESERVED_ANSWER>
<PRESERVED_CLASS>
The underlying task remains finding a 3-clique (triangle) in an undirected graph, keeping the time complexity requirement O(n*m/64) using bitset adjacency.
</PRESERVED_CLASS>
