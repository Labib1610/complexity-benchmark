# Problem C — Proof of Collusion

Time limit: 2 s
Memory limit: 256 MB

An auditor is attempting to divide $n$ regional suppliers into exactly two independent oversight tiers. However, certain pairs of suppliers share bilateral non-compete agreements. Two suppliers with such an agreement cannot be placed in the same oversight tier, as they would collude to manipulate the audit.

The auditor already knows that a two-tier division is hopeless. Your task is not to attempt the division, but to provide a concrete proof of its impossibility. You must find a supplier $x$ who shares an agreement with a supplier $y$, such that both $x$ and $y$ also share agreements with the same third supplier $z$. Because all three share agreements with one another, they cannot be separated into only two tiers without at least two of them ending up in the same tier. 

Output these three suppliers. It is guaranteed that the records contain at least one such configuration.

## Input
The first line contains two integers $n$ and $m$ ($3 \le n \le 2000$, $3 \le m \le \min(n \cdot (n-1)/2, 2 \cdot 10^5)$).
Each of the next $m$ lines contains two integers $u$ and $v$ — a pair of suppliers who share a bilateral agreement.
It is guaranteed that at least one configuration of three mutually agreeing suppliers exists.

## Output
Print three integers $a$, $b$, and $c$ on one line with $a < b < c$, representing the identifiers of the three suppliers. Output the lexicographically smallest such combination (smallest $a$; ties broken by smallest $b$; then smallest $c$).

## Example 1
Input:
```
4 5
1 2
1 3
2 3
2 4
3 4
```
Output:
```
1 2 3
```

## Example 2
Input:
```
5 6
1 2
1 4
2 4
3 4
3 5
4 5
```
Output:
```
1 2 4
```

This forces the model to trace the relationships to understand that it needs to find a triangle, rather than relying on standard graph terminology templates.
