<STATEMENT>
# Problem C — Proof of Collusion

Time limit: 2 s
Memory limit: 256 MB

An auditor is attempting to divide regional suppliers (identified by integers $1$ through $n$) into exactly two independent oversight tiers. However, the records contain bilateral non-compete agreements, each between a specific pair of suppliers. Two suppliers sharing such an agreement cannot be placed in the same oversight tier, as they would collude to manipulate the audit.

The auditor already knows that a two-tier division is hopeless for the given records. Your task is not to attempt the division, but to provide a concrete proof of its impossibility. To do this, you must locate a specific deadlock configuration involving exactly three distinct suppliers. The configuration requires that some two of these suppliers share an agreement, and the remaining third supplier shares an agreement with each of those first two. This creates an unresolvable conflict, preventing the trio from being split across just two tiers.

Output the identifiers of these three suppliers. It is guaranteed that the records contain at least one such configuration.

## Input
The first line contains two integers $n$ and $m$. The supplier headcount $n$ starts at a minimum of three and is capped by the corporate roster limit of exactly two thousand. The agreement count $m$ is at least three. Its upper bound is strictly limited by either the theoretical maximum of unique pairs among the $n$ suppliers, or the system's hard capacity of exactly two hundred thousand agreements, whichever is lower.
Each of the next $m$ lines contains two integers $u$ and $v$ — a pair of suppliers who share a bilateral agreement. (Each pair appears at most once in the records).
It is guaranteed that at least one trio of suppliers satisfying the proof condition exists.

## Output
Print three integers $a$, $b$, and $c$ on one line with $a < b < c$, representing the identifiers of the three suppliers. Output the lexicographically smallest such combination (smallest $a$; ties broken by smallest $b$; then smallest $c$).

## Example 1
Input:
```
4 5
1 2
1 3
2 3
2 4
3 4
```
Output:
```
1 2 3
```

## Example 2
Input:
```
5 6
1 2
1 4
2 4
3 4
3 5
4 5
```
Output:
```
1 2 4
```

The problem previously defined the required certificate symmetrically ("three distinct agreements... involve exactly three distinct suppliers"), which easily maps to the definition of a 3-clique. I replaced this with an asymmetric condition ("two of these suppliers share an agreement, and the remaining third supplier shares an agreement with each of those first two"). This forces the model to logically deduce that this configuration is topologically identical to a triangle, rather than relying on a direct keyword match. I also applied S07 by writing the numeric bounds as natural-language constraints tied to the scenario.
</RATIONALE>
