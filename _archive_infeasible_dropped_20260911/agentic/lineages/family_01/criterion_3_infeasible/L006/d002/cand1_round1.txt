<STATEMENT>
# Problem C — Proof of Collusion

Time limit: 2 s
Memory limit: 256 MB

An auditor is attempting to divide regional suppliers into exactly two independent oversight tiers. However, certain pairs of suppliers share bilateral non-compete agreements. Two suppliers with such an agreement cannot be placed in the same oversight tier, as they would collude to manipulate the audit.

The auditor already knows that a two-tier division is hopeless. Your task is not to attempt the division, but to provide a concrete proof of its impossibility. You must find a group of three distinct suppliers where every supplier in the group has a bilateral agreement with both of the other suppliers in that same group. Because each member of this trio is restricted from sharing a tier with either of the other two, they cannot be separated into only two tiers without a collision.

Output the identifiers of these three suppliers. It is guaranteed that the records contain at least one such configuration.

## Input
The first line contains two integers $n$ and $m$, representing the headcount of suppliers and the volume of bilateral agreements, respectively. The supplier headcount $n$ requires at least three to trigger an audit and is strictly capped by a system limit of two thousand regional offices. The agreement volume $m$ holds a minimum of three records, and its storage quota caps out at two hundred thousand entries (or the total number of unique supplier pairs, whichever is smaller).
Each of the next $m$ lines contains two integers $u$ and $v$ — a pair of suppliers who share a bilateral agreement.
It is guaranteed that at least one configuration of three mutually restricted suppliers exists.

## Output
Print three integers $a$, $b$, and $c$ on one line with $a < b < c$, representing the identifiers of the three suppliers. Output the lexicographically smallest such combination (smallest $a$; ties broken by smallest $b$; then smallest $c$).

## Example 1
Input:
```
4 5
1 2
1 3
2 3
2 4
3 4
```
Output:
```
1 2 3
```

## Example 2
Input:
```
5 6
1 2
1 4
2 4
3 4
3 5
4 5
```
Output:
```
1 2 4
```

Removed the asymmetric description of the trio (two sharing an agreement, and both sharing with a third) which directly prompted edge-and-common-neighbor logic, replacing it with a symmetric description. Replaced bare inequality bounds with scenario-based limits like "system limit" and "storage quota". This forces models to independently deduce the triangle-finding algorithm rather than pattern-matching the problem description.
</RATIONALE>
<PRESERVED_ANSWER>
The problem still asks for the lexicographically smallest set of three mutually adjacent suppliers, which is identical to finding the lexicographically first triangle in the graph, so the answer for any given input is unchanged.
</PRESERVED_ANSWER>

The underlying task remains identifying a 3-cycle (triangle) in an undirected graph to prove the impossibility of 2-coloring, preserving the original complexity class.
</PRESERVED_CLASS>
