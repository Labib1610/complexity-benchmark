<STATEMENT>
# Problem C — Proof of Collusion

Time limit: 2 s
Memory limit: 256 MB

An auditor is attempting to divide regional suppliers (identified by integers $1$ through $n$) into exactly two independent oversight tiers. The records contain bilateral non-compete agreements, each between a specific pair of suppliers. If two suppliers share such an agreement, they cannot be placed in the same oversight tier, as they would collude to manipulate the audit.

The auditor already knows that a two-tier division for the entire set of suppliers is hopeless. Your task is not to attempt the division, but to provide a concrete proof of its impossibility by identifying a localized deadlock. 

Specifically, you must find a set of exactly three distinct suppliers $a$, $b$, and $c$ (where $a < b < c$) such that, considering only these three suppliers and the bilateral agreements that exist among them, it is strictly impossible to assign them to two oversight tiers without placing at least one pair of suppliers who share an agreement in the same tier. 

Output the identifiers of these three suppliers. It is guaranteed that the records contain at least one such trio.

## Input
The first line contains two integers $n$ and $m$ ($3 \le n \le 2000$, $3 \le m \le \min(n(n-1)/2, 200000)$).
Each of the next $m$ lines contains two integers $u$ and $v$ — a pair of suppliers who share a bilateral agreement.
It is guaranteed that at least one trio of suppliers satisfying the proof condition exists.

## Output
Print three integers $a$, $b$, and $c$ on one line with $a < b < c$, representing the identifiers of the three suppliers. Output the lexicographically smallest such combination (smallest $a$; ties broken by smallest $b$; then smallest $c$).

## Example 1
Input:
```
4 5
1 2
1 3
2 3
2 4
3 4
```
Output:
```
1 2 3
```

## Example 2
Input:
```
5 6
1 2
1 4
2 4
3 4
3 5
4 5
```
Output:
```
1 2 4
```
</STATEMENT>
<RATIONALE>
Removed the explicit list of the three required edges (a-b, b-c, a-c) that models were using to trivially pattern-match the problem to 3-clique/triangle finding. Replaced it with the mathematically equivalent condition that the three vertices cannot be validly 2-colored. Models must now genuinely reason about graph properties (realizing that a non-2-colorable graph on 3 vertices must be a triangle) to arrive at the optimized bitset algorithm, otherwise a literal translation of the rules yields an $O(N^3)$ brute force that will Time Limit Exceed.
</RATIONALE>
<PRESERVED_ANSWER>
A set of exactly three vertices cannot be validly partitioned into two independent sets if and only if it contains all three possible edges between them (a triangle), meaning the exact same trios are valid as in the seed problem.
</PRESERVED_ANSWER>
<PRESERVED_CLASS>
The underlying mathematical task remains exactly finding a 3-clique (triangle) in an undirected graph.
</PRESERVED_CLASS>
<RISK>
A model might misinterpret "considering only these three suppliers and the bilateral agreements that exist among them" as meaning it needs to check if the three nodes are disconnected from the rest of the graph, though the phrase "considering only" typically implies ignoring external edges rather than forbidding them.
</RISK>
