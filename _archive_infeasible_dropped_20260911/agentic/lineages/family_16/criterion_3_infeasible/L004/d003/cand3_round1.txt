<STATEMENT>
# Problem C — Impossible Deadline: Certify It

Time limit: 2 s   Memory limit: 256 MB

## Description

A manufacturing facility processes **j** orders using two specialized workstations. Each order must first be processed at workstation 1 and subsequently at workstation 2. Each workstation can handle only one order at a time, and an order cannot begin at workstation 2 until its processing at workstation 1 is complete. For each order, you are given its required processing time at workstation 1 ($p1$) and workstation 2 ($p2$).

All orders must be fully completed by a strict deadline **T**. 

For all provided cases, it is guaranteed that meeting the deadline **T** is impossible regardless of the processing sequence. Your job is not to search for a schedule; it is to prove impossibility with a short certificate checkable from the input. The certificate must identify a physical bottleneck that independently prevents completion within the deadline. A valid bottleneck is either a single order's mandatory sequential timeline across both workstations (output `JOB i`, where **i** is the 1-based order index), or a single workstation's cumulative busy interval across all assigned work (output `LOAD c`, where **c** is the workstation number 1 or 2). The input is constructed such that exactly one such bottleneck exists. Print the one that holds.

## Input

The first line contains two integers **j** and **T**. The number of orders **j** is at least one, and is physically limited by the facility's inventory of exactly 200,000 transport pallets (each order requires exactly one pallet). The deadline **T** is measured in nanoseconds, is at least 1, and can be up to exactly one full second (which implies the maximum possible value of **T**).
Each of the next **j** lines contains two integers **p1** and **p2**: the processing times of an order on workstation 1 and workstation 2, both given in nanoseconds. Each operation requires at least 1 nanosecond, and is strictly limited by a hardware watchdog timer to a maximum of one millisecond (which implies the maximum possible value for a single processing time).

## Output

Print a single line containing the certificate of impossibility: either `JOB i` (where i is the order index) or `LOAD c` (where c is the workstation number).

## Examples

### Example 1

**Input**
```
3 100
70 60
2 2
3 4
```

**Output**
```
JOB 1
```

### Example 2

**Input**
```
4 150
50 1
50 1
50 1
50 1
```

**Output**
```
LOAD 1
```

## Note

In Example 1, even if the facility only had to process the first order, it would still fail to meet the 100-nanosecond deadline because of its mandatory sequential timeline across the two workstations (70 then 60, total 130). Thus, `JOB 1` proves the deadline is impossible. In Example 2, no single order violates the deadline on its own, but the first workstation's cumulative busy interval across all assigned work requires 200 nanoseconds overall. Since it processes tasks one at a time, it cannot finish within the 150-nanosecond deadline, making `LOAD 1` the valid certificate.
</STATEMENT>

Removed the explicit mathematical inequalities (`p1_i + p2_i > T`, total sum > T) and replaced them with descriptive operational requirements ("mandatory sequential timeline", "cumulative busy interval"). Applied Strategy S07 by hiding the maximum bounds for `T` and the processing times behind unit conversions (seconds and milliseconds to nanoseconds), forcing the model to derive the numeric bounds rather than parse them directly. This targets models relying on syntactic pattern matching of formulas.
</RATIONALE>
