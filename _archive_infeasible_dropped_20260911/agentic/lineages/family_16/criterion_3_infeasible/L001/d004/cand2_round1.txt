# Problem C — Impossibility Certificate

Time limit: 2 s   Memory limit: 256 MB

## Description

A factory is tasked with completing **j** distinct manufactured items. The production of each item requires sequential processing through two specialized departments: Department 1 must completely finish an item before its Department 2 processing can begin. You are provided with the required processing duration in Department 1 and Department 2 for every item. Neither department can process more than one item simultaneously.

The factory operates under a strict global deadline **T**. All operations across both departments for all items must be finalized by this time.

In this specific problem, **no valid schedule exists** that can meet the deadline **T** for the given inputs. Instead of attempting to schedule the items, your task is to output a simple proof (a certificate) demonstrating why the deadline is structurally impossible to meet. 

It is guaranteed that exactly one of the two relaxations defined below will independently prove the impossibility for each testcase. You must determine which relaxation fails and output a valid certificate for it. If the applicable relaxation offers multiple valid certificates, any single one is acceptable.

The acceptable certificates are:

- `JOB i` (where **i** is the 1-based index of the item): Demonstrates that the deadline is impossible by isolating item **i**. This certificate is valid if no schedule could meet the deadline even if all other **j-1** items required zero processing time in both departments.
  
- `LOAD c` (where **c** is the department number, either 1 or 2): Demonstrates that the deadline is impossible by isolating Department **c**. This certificate is valid if no schedule could meet the deadline even if the other department required zero processing time for all items.

## Input

The first line specifies two integers **j** and **T** (1 ≤ j ≤ 200000, 1 ≤ T ≤ 1000000000).
The subsequent **j** lines each provide two integers **p1** and **p2** (1 ≤ p1, p2 ≤ 1000000), representing the processing duration required for that item in Department 1 and Department 2, respectively.

## Output

Output exactly one line containing a valid certificate from the applicable relaxation: either `JOB i` or `LOAD c`.

## Examples

### Example 1

**Input**
```
3 100
70 60
2 2
3 4
```

**Output**
```
JOB 1
```

### Example 2

**Input**
```
4 150
50 1
50 1
50 1
50 1
```

**Output**
```
LOAD 1
```

## Note

In Example 1, if items 2 and 3 required zero time, item 1 still must pass through Department 1 (70 units) and then Department 2 (60 units) sequentially. Its minimum possible completion time is 130, which strictly exceeds the deadline of 100, making `JOB 1` a valid certificate.

In Example 2, if Department 2 required zero processing time for all items, Department 1 is still required to do 200 total units of work (50 for each of the 4 items). Since a department processes one item at a time, Department 1 cannot finish its workload within the 150 deadline, making `LOAD 1` a valid certificate.
</STATEMENT>
<RATIONALE>
Removed the explicit mathematical inequalities for `JOB i` (`p1 + p2 > T`) and `LOAD c` (`sum(p_c) > T`) that the models were directly transcribing. Replaced them with equivalent logical definitions framed as scheduling relaxations (setting other durations to zero). This forces the model to actually analyze the physical constraints of the factory to deduce the required calculations.
</RATIONALE>
<PRESERVED_ANSWER>
The logical conditions remain exactly identical: an item missing the deadline when all others take zero time mathematically evaluates to `p1_i + p2_i > T`, and a department missing the deadline when the other takes zero time evaluates to `sum(p_c) > T`.
</PRESERVED_ANSWER>
<PRESERVED_CLASS>
The complexity class remains exactly the same, as the required checks are still single-pass linear scans over the input arrays.
</PRESERVED_CLASS>
<RISK>
Models might misinterpret the relaxation as requiring a full scheduling simulation rather than simplifying to the straightforward sum, though the examples explicitly walk through the sum logic.
</RISK>
