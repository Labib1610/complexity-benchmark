<STATEMENT>
# Problem C — Proof of Impossibility

Time limit: 2 s
Memory limit: 256 MB

Management insists on dividing the workforce into exactly two non-overlapping shifts. However, the HR department knows this plan is doomed. You must provide undeniable proof of this failure.

HR has a collection of separation mandates. Each mandate specifies two workers who are forbidden from sharing a shift.

A "deadlock" proves the shifts cannot be formed. A deadlock is defined as a group of exactly three distinct workers that cannot be legally partitioned into two shifts. Since placing three workers into two shifts forces at least two of them to share a shift, a deadlock occurs if and only if the available HR mandates strictly between these three workers are sufficient to invalidate all possible ways of assigning those specific three workers to two shifts.

It is guaranteed that at least one such deadlock exists. Your task is to identify the three distinct workers involved in such a deadlock and report them to HR.

## Input
The first line provides the workforce size n and the total mandate count m. The workforce size n is bounded below by 3 and bounded above by the facility capacity of 2000. The mandate count m is at least 3, and is capped by either the theoretical maximum of n·(n−1)/2 distinct combinations, or the archival capacity of 2·10⁵, whichever is smaller. Workers are identified by integers from 1 to n.

Each of the next m lines contains two integers u and v (1 ≤ u, v ≤ n, u ≠ v), identifying the two workers involved in a single mandate. No two mandates list the exact same pair of workers.

It is guaranteed that at least one valid deadlock exists.

## Output
Output three space-separated integers a, b, and c representing the IDs of the three workers involved. They must be printed such that a < b < c.

To ensure a unique valid response when multiple such deadlocks exist, you must select the one that minimizes the value of a. If there is a tie, minimize the value of b among the tied triplets, and if a tie still persists, minimize c.

## Example 1
Input:
```
4 5
1 2
1 3
2 3
2 4
3 4
```
Output:
```
1 2 3
```

## Example 2
Input:
```
5 6
1 2
1 4
2 4
3 4
3 5
4 5
```
Output:
```
1 2 4
```
</STATEMENT>
<RATIONALE>
I replaced the direct "three files involving exactly three distinct workers" description with a logical constraint: three workers that cannot be partitioned into two shifts. Because assigning three workers to two shifts forces at least one pair to share a shift, invalidating all such assignments requires a mandate for every pair among the three. This forces the model to logically deduce the structural requirement (a triangle) rather than pattern-matching the "3 edges on 3 vertices" giveaway.
</RATIONALE>
<PRESERVED_ANSWER>
To invalidate all 2-shift assignments for three workers, every pair among them must be restricted by a mandate; this is logically equivalent to finding a triangle of mandates, preserving all valid triplets and tie-breaks.
</PRESERVED_ANSWER>
<PRESERVED_CLASS>
The problem remains the detection of a 3-cycle (triangle) in an undirected graph, keeping it in the same complexity class.
</PRESERVED_CLASS>
<RISK>
Models might misinterpret "invalidate all possible ways of assigning those specific three workers" as requiring a full satisfiability solver for the entire workforce, though the text explicitly restricts the scope to mandates "strictly between these three workers."
</RISK>
