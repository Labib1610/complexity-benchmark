Algebraic Edge Counting
</METHOD_NAME>
<METHOD_DESCRIPTION>
Replaces the "impossible to 2-color" description of a triangle with a mathematical condition on a subset of 3 vertices: X - Y = 3, where X is the number of edges with at least one endpoint in S, and Y is the number of edges with exactly one endpoint in S. Models must algebraically deduce that X - Y simplifies to the number of internal edges in S, which means the 3 vertices form a triangle. Without this deduction, literal implementation of X and Y counting takes O(N^3 * M) and results in a Time Limit Exceeded.
</METHOD_DESCRIPTION>

<STATEMENT>
# Critical Interference

Time limit: 2 s
Memory limit: 256 MB

A proposed communications standard attempts to allocate $n$ transceivers (labeled 1 through $n$) into a network. Testing has revealed $m$ specific bidirectional interference links between pairs of transceivers. 

Your objective is to isolate a specific structural flaw in the network. You must locate a subset $S$ of exactly three transceivers that satisfies a critical density condition.

For any subset of transceivers $S$, we define two quantities based on the interference links:
- $X$: the total number of interference links where at least one of the two linked transceivers is in $S$.
- $Y$: the total number of interference links where exactly one of the two linked transceivers is in $S$.

You must find a subset $S$ of exactly three transceivers such that $X - Y = 3$. 

The existence of such a subset indicates a localized failure in the network topology. It is guaranteed that at least one such subset is present in the network.

## Input
The first line contains the transceiver count $n$ and the interference link count $m$ ($3 \le n \le 2000$, $3 \le m \le \min(n \cdot (n-1)/2, 2 \cdot 10^5)$).
The following $m$ lines each contain two integers $u$ and $v$, representing a pair of transceivers that mutually interfere.
At least one subset of three transceivers satisfying the condition is guaranteed to exist.

## Output
Provide the labels of the three transceivers forming the subset, printed on a single line in ascending numerical order.
In the event that several such subsets exist, you must resolve the ambiguity by selecting the subset that includes the transceiver with the absolute lowest label. Should multiple subsets share this lowest-labeled transceiver, further narrow your selection to those whose second-lowest label is as small as possible. If ambiguity still persists, choose the subset whose highest label is minimal.

## Example 1
### Input
```
4 5
1 2
1 3
2 3
2 4
3 4
```
### Output
```
1 2 3
```

## Example 2
### Input
```
5 6
1 2
1 4
2 4
3 4
3 5
4 5
```
### Output
```
1 2 4
```

<RATIONALE>
I replaced the bipartite coloring impossibility condition with an algebraic edge-counting property ($X - Y = 3$). This forces the model to mathematically deduce that $X - Y$ simplifies to the number of internal edges in $S$, meaning the 3 vertices form a triangle, rather than relying on standard graph-coloring terminology.
</RATIONALE>
<PRESERVED_ANSWER>
By inclusion-exclusion, $X - Y$ exactly equals the number of edges with both endpoints in $S$; thus $X - Y = 3$ for a subset of size 3 is mathematically identical to finding a triangle, preserving all valid subsets and the tie-breaking logic.
</PRESERVED_ANSWER>
