<METHOD_DESCRIPTION>Replaces the explicit description of a mutually interfering clique with a purely combinatorial condition on subsets and internal edge counts. Models matching "every transceiver interferes with the other two" will fail to trigger on "subset of size 3 with exactly 3 internal edges", forcing them to either naively simulate the subset condition (which results in TLE) or genuinely deduce the geometric equivalence to a triangle.</METHOD_DESCRIPTION>

<STATEMENT>
# Critical Interference

Time limit: 2 s
Memory limit: 256 MB

A proposed communications standard attempts to allocate $n$ transceivers (labeled 1 through $n$) into exactly two distinct frequency bands. However, testing has revealed $m$ specific bidirectional interference links between pairs of transceivers. If two linked transceivers operate on the same frequency band, the signal degrades entirely.

The standard's allocation constraints are fundamentally impossible to satisfy. Your objective is to isolate the root cause of this failure. Specifically, you must locate a subset of exactly three transceivers such that the number of given interference links that connect two transceivers within this subset is exactly three.

The existence of such a subset proves that two frequency bands are not enough. It is guaranteed that at least one such subset is present in the network.

## Input
The first line contains the transceiver count $n$ and the interference link count $m$ ($3 \le n \le 2000$, $3 \le m \le \min(n \cdot (n-1)/2, 2 \cdot 10^5)$).
The following $m$ lines each detail a distinct pair of transceivers $u$ and $v$ that mutually interfere.
At least one valid subset of exactly three transceivers is guaranteed to exist.

## Output
Provide the labels of the three transceivers forming the subset, printed on a single line in ascending numerical order.
In the event that several such subsets exist, you must resolve the ambiguity by selecting the subset that includes the transceiver with the absolute lowest label. Should multiple subsets share this lowest-labeled transceiver, further narrow your selection to those whose second-lowest label is as small as possible. If ambiguity still persists, choose the subset whose highest label is minimal.

## Example 1
### Input
```
4 5
1 2
1 3
2 3
2 4
3 4
```
### Output
```
1 2 3
```

## Example 2
### Input
```
5 6
1 2
1 4
2 4
3 4
3 5
4 5
```
### Output
```
1 2 4
```

<RATIONALE>
The explicit description of a clique ("cluster wherein every transceiver interferes with the other two") was replaced with a combinatorial property ("subset of exactly three transceivers such that the number of given interference links that connect two transceivers within this subset is exactly three"). This forces models to deduce that a 3-node subset with 3 internal edges is geometrically equivalent to a triangle, preventing them from falling back on pre-memorized graph traversal templates without genuine reasoning.
</RATIONALE>
<PRESERVED_ANSWER>
A subset of three nodes can contain a maximum of three edges; thus, requiring exactly three internal edges is mathematically identical to requiring that all three nodes are mutually connected, preserving all valid answers and the tie-breaking logic.
</PRESERVED_ANSWER>
<PRESERVED_CLASS>
The problem still requires finding a 3-clique (triangle) in a graph, which remains solvable in the exact same time complexity using adjacency intersections or bitsets.
</PRESERVED_CLASS>
