<STATEMENT>
# Problem A — Corporate Integration

Time limit: 5 s   Memory limit: 256 MB

## Description

You are analyzing a market consisting of **n** corporations, numbered 1 through n. There are **m** potential joint ventures available for investment. Each joint venture involves exactly two distinct corporations and requires a specific initial capital investment to fund.

You are given a list of **t** target corporations. Your objective is to select a subset of the potential joint ventures to fund such that the target corporations become *integrated*.

A subset of funded joint ventures is considered to integrate the target corporations if it is **impossible** to divide the **n** corporations into two disjoint groups, $A$ and $B$ (where every corporation is assigned to exactly one group), such that all three of the following conditions are simultaneously true:
1. At least one target corporation is in group $A$.
2. At least one target corporation is in group $B$.
3. None of the funded joint ventures involve both a corporation in group $A$ and a corporation in group $B$.

You may fund joint ventures involving non-target corporations; they are subject to the same rules and can help satisfy the integration requirement.

Calculate the minimum possible total capital investment required to fund a subset of joint ventures that integrates all **t** target corporations.

## Input

The first line contains three integers **n**, **m**, and **t** (1 ≤ t ≤ 10, 1 ≤ n ≤ 100, 0 ≤ m ≤ n·(n−1)/2).
The second line contains **t** distinct integers: the identifiers of the target corporations.
Each of the next **m** lines contains three integers **u**, **v**, and **w** (1 ≤ u, v ≤ n, u ≠ v, 1 ≤ w ≤ 1000000): a potential joint venture between corporations u and v that requires w capital to fund.
The available joint ventures are sufficient to allow the target corporations to be integrated.

## Output

Print a single integer: the minimum total capital investment required.

## Examples

### Example 1

**Input**
```
4 6 3
1 2 3
1 4 1
2 4 1
3 4 1
1 2 2
2 3 2
1 3 2
```

**Output**
```
3
```

### Example 2

**Input**
```
4 4 3
1 2 3
1 2 1
2 3 1
1 4 10
3 4 10
```

**Output**
```
2
```

### Example 3

**Input**
```
3 2 1
1
1 2 5
2 3 5
```

**Output**
```
0
```

## Note

In Example 1, funding the joint ventures between corporation 4 and each of the three target corporations (total capital 1+1+1 = 3) successfully integrates targets 1, 2, and 3. If we attempt to divide the corporations such that the targets are separated (for instance, $A=\{1\}$ and $B=\{2, 3, 4\}$), the funded venture between 1 and 4 involves a corporation in $A$ and a corporation in $B$, which violates condition 3. This indirect integration is cheaper than funding ventures directly among the targets (which would cost 4). 
In Example 3, with only one target corporation, it is inherently impossible to place a target in $A$ and a target in $B$. The conditions are trivially impossible to meet, so no joint ventures need to be funded, and the minimum capital is 0.
</STATEMENT>
<RATIONALE>
I replaced the standard constructive definition of a connected component (a path of edges exists between all terminals) with its exact dual definition via cuts (no partition separates the terminals without intersecting a chosen edge). LLMs rely heavily on keyword association; presenting connectivity purely through negative cut conditions forces them to conceptually bridge the gap from "no valid cuts" to "minimum Steiner tree". This typically derails pattern-matching, causing models to attempt Min-Cut, Max-Cut, or Bipartite grouping algorithms instead of the required Dreyfus-Wagner subset DP.
</RATIONALE>
<PRESERVED_ANSWER>
By Menger's theorem and basic graph connectivity, it is impossible to cut a graph and separate a subset of vertices without cutting an edge if and only if those vertices all belong to the same connected component, meaning the problem still asks for the minimum weight subgraph connecting the target vertices.
</PRESERVED_ANSWER>
<PRESERVED_CLASS>
The problem remains exactly the Minimum Steiner Tree problem on an undirected graph, which is NP-complete.
</PRESERVED_CLASS>
<RISK>
Models or readers might misunderstand the "impossible to divide" condition as a requirement for the graph to be globally strongly connected or non-bipartite, but the explicit conditions (specifically condition 1 and 2 requiring targets in both groups) strictly limit the impossibility to cuts that separate the targets.
</RISK>
