<STATEMENT>
# Problem A — Relic Restoration

Time limit: 5 s
Memory limit: 256 MB

## Description
A museum is funding the restoration of a shattered historical artifact. The artifact is currently broken into $n$ distinct fragments, numbered 1 to $n$. 

The curators have identified $t$ critical fragments that contain pieces of a continuous ancient inscription. The primary goal of the restoration is to ensure that these $t$ critical fragments are unified into a single, solid assembly so the inscription can be studied.

The restoration team has scanned the fragments and found $m$ valid contact surfaces where two specific fragments can be seamlessly bonded. Bonding fragment $u$ and fragment $v$ requires applying a specific amount of specialized resin, which costs $w$ drops. 

When fragments are bonded, they form a larger solid unit. This property is transitive: if fragment $A$ is bonded to fragment $B$, and fragment $B$ is bonded to fragment $C$, all three become part of the same solid unit. 

You may choose to bond any combination of fragments, including incorporating non-critical fragments into the assembly, provided it minimizes the overall resin used. You must calculate the minimum total drops of resin required to ensure all $t$ critical fragments belong to the exact same solid unit.

## Input
The first line contains three integers $n$, $m$, and $t$ ($1 \le t \le 10$, $1 \le n \le 100$, $0 \le m \le n(n-1)/2$).
The second line contains $t$ distinct integers: the IDs of the critical fragments.
Each of the next $m$ lines contains three integers $u$, $v$, and $w$ ($1 \le u, v \le n$, $u \neq v$, $1 \le w \le 1000000$), representing a valid contact surface between fragment $u$ and fragment $v$ that requires $w$ drops of resin to bond.
It is guaranteed that the available contact surfaces are sufficient to unify all critical fragments together.

## Output
Print a single integer: the minimum total drops of resin required to ensure all critical fragments belong to one solid unit.

## Examples

### Example 1

**Input**
```
4 6 3
1 2 3
1 4 1
2 4 1
3 4 1
1 2 2
2 3 2
1 3 2
```

**Output**
```
3
```

### Example 2

**Input**
```
4 4 3
1 2 3
1 2 1
2 3 1
1 4 10
3 4 10
```

**Output**
```
2
```

### Example 3

**Input**
```
3 2 1
1
1 2 5
2 3 5
```

**Output**
```
0
```

## Note
In Example 1, bonding the critical fragments 1, 2, and 3 to the non-critical fragment 4 (which costs 1+1+1 = 3 drops of resin) is more efficient than bonding the three critical fragments directly to each other (which would cost 4 drops). 
In Example 3, there is only one critical fragment, so no bonding is necessary.

The problem was completely reframed from a network infrastructure scenario ("fiber-optic links", "passive relays") to a physical object assembly scenario ("fragments", "resin", "solid unit"). This removes obvious networking trigger words that models use to immediately retrieve the standard Steiner Tree dynamic programming setup, forcing them to map the transitive bonding of fragments to graph connectivity on their own.
</RATIONALE>

The mathematical structure is identical: finding the minimum weight subset of edges (resin drops) to ensure a specific subset of vertices (critical fragments) belong to the same connected component (solid unit).
</PRESERVED_ANSWER>

The problem remains the classic Steiner Tree on graphs, which is NP-complete.
