# Policy Reconciliation

Time limit: 2 s   Memory limit: 256 MB

## Description

A legal database contains $R$ clauses (numbered 1 to $R$). There are $S$ established cross-references between specific pairs of clauses.

A new policy document must be drafted by selecting a subset of these $R$ clauses. To be valid, the selected subset must satisfy two conditions:
1. It must include all $P$ of the mandatory clauses (it may also include any number of non-mandatory clauses).
2. The selected subset must be *coherent*. A subset of clauses is considered coherent if it is impossible to divide its clauses into two disjoint, non-empty groups (which together contain all clauses in the subset) such that there are zero cross-references connecting a clause in the first group to a clause in the second group.

When you include clauses in your document, any existing cross-reference that connects two clauses that are *both* in your document becomes "active". Cross-references that connect an included clause to an omitted clause, or two omitted clauses, remain inactive.

Each cross-reference has a specific reconciliation cost. The total cost of your policy document is the sum of the reconciliation costs of all active cross-references. Find the minimum possible total cost to form a valid policy document.

## Input

The first line contains three integers $R$, $S$, and $P$. 
The total number of clauses $R$ satisfies $1 \le R \le 200,000$. 
The number of cross-references $S$ is exactly $R - 1$. 
The number of mandatory clauses $P$ satisfies $1 \le P \le R$.

The second line contains $P$ distinct integers: the identifiers of the mandatory clauses.

Each of the next $S$ lines contains three integers $u$, $v$, and $w$ ($1 \le u, v \le R$, $u \neq v$). This represents a cross-reference between clause $u$ and clause $v$ with a reconciliation cost $w$ ($1 \le w \le 1,000,000$). 

The cross-references provided in the input are guaranteed to make the full database of $R$ clauses coherent. Furthermore, if any single cross-reference were permanently removed, the database would immediately lose this property. This strict balance guarantees that $S$ is exactly $R - 1$.

## Output

Print a single integer: the minimum total reconciliation cost of a valid policy document.

<RATIONALE>
The problem was completely reframed from a physical/navigational network to a legal database. Crucially, the standard graph-theory vocabulary ("connected component", "travel", "route") was replaced with its mathematical contrapositive: the inability to partition a set into two disconnected groups. This forces the model to logically deduce that "coherence" means "forms a connected subgraph" and that the input guarantees describe a spanning tree, rather than relying on keyword pattern matching to retrieve a tree traversal algorithm.
</RATIONALE>
<PRESERVED_ANSWER>
The requirement to select a "coherent" subset of clauses translates exactly to selecting a connected induced subgraph, and minimizing the cost of active internal edges on a tree still mathematically equates to finding the weight of the unique minimal subtree spanning the mandatory nodes.
</PRESERVED_ANSWER>
<PRESERVED_CLASS>
The underlying graph is still a tree and the task is still to find the minimal spanning subtree for a set of terminals, which remains solvable in O(R) time and keeps the problem firmly in P.
</PRESERVED_CLASS>
<RISK>
Models might struggle to parse the definition of "coherent" if they misinterpret "impossible to divide... into two disjoint, non-empty groups" for subsets of size 1, though mathematically a set of size 1 trivially cannot be divided into two non-empty groups, making it inherently coherent.
</RISK>
