# Problem B — Microfluidic Chip Optimization

Time limit: 2 s   Memory limit: 256 MB

## Description

You are tasked with optimizing a microfluidic chip containing **n** fluid chambers, numbered 1 to **n**, and **m** two-way capillary tubes. Each tube connects two distinct chambers and has a known fluid capacity.

We say a subset of chambers is *hydrostatically locked* if there is no way to divide it into two non-empty subsets without at least one capillary tube connecting a chamber in the first subset to a chamber in the second. (A single chamber cannot be divided and is inherently locked). The initial chip is fully locked. Because the entire chip is initially locked and contains exactly **m = n − 1** tubes, there is exactly one sequence of tubes linking any two given chambers.

You may perform a sequence of chamber sealings to optimize the chip. In a single step, you permanently seal exactly one chamber, which automatically removes any capillary tubes connected to it. However, your sealing sequence must obey two strict safety regulations:
1. You may never seal any of the **t** *extraction chambers*, as they are required for the final output.
2. After every individual sealing step, the remaining unsealed chambers must still form a *hydrostatically locked* subset. If sealing a particular chamber would cause the remaining unsealed chambers to lose their lock (meaning they could then be divided into two disconnected subsets), you cannot seal that chamber at that time.

You can halt the sealing process at any point, or choose to make no sealings at all. Your objective is to determine the minimum possible total fluid capacity of the capillary tubes that remain unsealed when you finish.

## Input

The first line provides three integers **n**, **m**, and **t** (1 ≤ t ≤ n, 1 ≤ n ≤ 200000, m = n − 1).
The second line provides **t** distinct integers representing the labels of the extraction chambers.
Each of the subsequent **m** lines provides three integers **u**, **v**, and **w** (1 ≤ u, v ≤ n, u ≠ v, 1 ≤ w ≤ 1000000), indicating a capillary tube between chamber **u** and chamber **v** with a fluid capacity of **w**.

## Output

Output a single integer representing the minimum possible total fluid capacity of the remaining capillary tubes after a valid sequence of sealings.

<METHOD_NAME>Physical State Translation</METHOD_NAME>
<METHOD_DESCRIPTION>Replaces abstract graph terminology with a concrete physical system (microfluidics) where operations (node deletion) are physical actions (sealing chambers) and properties (connectivity) are physical states (hydrostatically locked). This prevents simple keyword-based mapping to standard graph algorithms like Steiner tree or leaf pruning, forcing the model to correctly interpret the physical rules to derive the underlying tree structure.</METHOD_DESCRIPTION>
<METHOD_NOVEL>yes</METHOD_NOVEL>
<RATIONALE>
The abstract variables and coupling constraints have been replaced with a physical microfluidic chip model. By framing the connectivity as "hydrostatically locked" and node deletion as "chamber sealing", models cannot rely on standard graph theory keywords. They must deduce that the physical rules describe a tree and that valid sealings correspond to pruning non-target leaves.
</RATIONALE>
<PRESERVED_ANSWER>
The operations and constraints are isomorphic to the original: sealing a chamber is exactly dropping a vertex and its incident edges, and the hydrostatic lock rule perfectly preserves the connectivity requirement, so the minimal subgraph cost remains exactly the same.
</PRESERVED_ANSWER>
<PRESERVED_CLASS>
The problem still asks for the minimum connected subgraph containing a set of target vertices in a given tree, which is exactly the unique minimal Steiner tree and computable in linear time (P).
</PRESERVED_CLASS>
<RISK>
Models might misinterpret "removes any capillary tubes connected to it" as modifying the capacities rather than deleting the edges, though "permanently seal" and "removes" strongly imply graph deletion.
</RISK>
