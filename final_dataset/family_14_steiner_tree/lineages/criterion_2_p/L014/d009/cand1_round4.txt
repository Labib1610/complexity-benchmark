<STATEMENT>
# Corporate Consolidation

Time limit: 2 s   Memory limit: 256 MB

## Description

In a regulated market, there are $n$ independent corporations (numbered 1 through $n$) and a set of $m$ proposed bilateral merger agreements. Each agreement involves two specific corporations and requires a defined legal fee to finalize.

Within this market, a particular group of $t$ corporations are identified as key patent holders. 

To maintain market equilibrium, the antitrust regulatory board has issued a certificate that enforces strict structural limits on the proposed agreements. The certificate guarantees that the market is fully integrated without any redundancy: between any two corporations, there is exactly one valid sequence of proposed agreements that links them. Consequently, the total number of proposed agreements, $m$, is always exactly one fewer than the number of corporations.

Instead of finalizing all agreements, the board seeks to eliminate unnecessary bureaucracy through a step-by-step reduction process. 

At any point during this process, a corporation is considered *marginal* if it is not a key patent holder and it is involved in exactly one currently remaining proposed agreement. The single remaining agreement involving a marginal corporation is considered *extraneous*.

The reduction process proceeds as follows:
1. Identify any extraneous agreement.
2. Cancel it (remove it from the set of currently remaining agreements).
3. Repeat this process until no extraneous agreements remain.

The agreements that survive this process are designated as the *core agreements*. (Because of the market's structural limits, the final set of core agreements is unique and does not depend on the order of cancellation).

Your goal is to calculate the total legal fee of all the *core agreements*.

## Input

The first line contains three integers $n$, $m$, and $t$ ($1 \le t \le n$, $1 \le n \le 200000$).
The second line contains $t$ distinct integers: the identifiers of the key patent holders.
Each of the next $m$ lines contains three integers $u$, $v$, and $w$ ($1 \le u, v \le n$, $u \neq v$, $1 \le w \le 1000000$), representing a proposed agreement between corporation $u$ and corporation $v$ with a legal fee of $w$.

## Output

Print a single integer: the total legal fee of all the core agreements.

## Examples

### Example 1

**Input**
```
4 3 2
1 4
1 2 5
2 3 1
3 4 2
```

**Output**
```
8
```

### Example 2

**Input**
```
5 4 1
3
1 2 4
2 3 6
3 4 2
4 5 9
```

**Output**
```
0
```

### Example 3

**Input**
```
3 2 3
1 2 3
1 2 4
2 3 5
```

**Output**
```
9
```

## Note

In Example 1, the key patent holders are 1 and 4. 
Initially, the corporations involved in exactly one agreement are 1 and 4. However, both are key patent holders, so neither is a marginal corporation.
Because there are no marginal corporations, there are no extraneous agreements. The reduction process terminates immediately.
All three agreements survive and are designated as core agreements. The total legal fee is 5 + 1 + 2 = 8. 

In Example 2, there is only one key patent holder (corporation 3). 
Initially, corporations 1 and 5 are involved in exactly one agreement each (agreements between 1 and 2, and between 4 and 5, respectively). Neither is a key patent holder, so both are marginal.
We can cancel the extraneous agreement between 1 and 2. Now corporation 2 is involved in only the remaining agreement between 2 and 3. Since 2 is not a key patent holder, this agreement is now extraneous and can be canceled.
Similarly, canceling the agreement between 4 and 5 leaves corporation 4 involved only in the agreement between 3 and 4. Since 4 is not a key patent holder, this agreement becomes extraneous and is canceled.
Eventually, all agreements are canceled. There are no core agreements, resulting in a total legal fee of 0.

In Example 3, the key patent holders are 1, 2, and 3.
Corporations 1 and 3 are initially involved in exactly one agreement. However, they are both key patent holders, so they are not marginal.
There are no extraneous agreements. Both agreements are core agreements. The total legal fee is 4 + 5 = 9.

<RATIONALE>
I replaced the seed's edge-cut separation condition (which models easily map to subtree key counts) with a mathematically equivalent iterative leaf-pruning process, while fully restoring the explicit structural tree guarantees to satisfy the expert critique and preserve the exact domain. This forces models to either recognize the mathematical equivalence to the minimal subtree (and use the O(n) DFS) or correctly implement the pruning in O(n) time, causing surface-pattern matchers to either fail the implementation or write a naive O(n^2) simulation that times out.
</RATIONALE>
<PRESERVED_ANSWER>
In a tree, iteratively pruning leaf nodes that are not required key patent holders exactly yields the minimal subtree spanning the required nodes, which perfectly matches the set of critical agreements defined by edge-cuts in the seed problem.
</PRESERVED_ANSWER>
<PRESERVED_CLASS>
The core agreements can still be found in O(n) time either by standard DFS (computing subtree counts) or by using a queue to prune leaves, keeping the optimal complexity unchanged in P.
</PRESERVED_CLASS>
<RISK>
Models might misinterpret "involved in exactly one currently remaining proposed agreement" as applying to the initial state only, though the word "currently" and the step-by-step nature of the reduction process strongly clarify it is dynamic.
</RISK>
