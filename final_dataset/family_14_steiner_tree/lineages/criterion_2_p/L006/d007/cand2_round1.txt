<METHOD_DESCRIPTION>Replaces graph/cut terminology with an accounting scenario where nodes are assigned to one of two tax jurisdictions. The cut condition is reframed as a regulatory property requiring cross-jurisdiction reconciliation procedures. This obfuscates the spatial tree structure and forces the model to independently deduce that the 2-coloring constraint is equivalent to maintaining connectivity among the flagged accounts.</METHOD_DESCRIPTION>

<STATEMENT>
# Problem B — Financial Reconciliation

Time limit: 2 s
Memory limit: 256 MB

## Description

You are auditing a corporate ledger consisting of $n$ financial accounts, numbered 1 through $n$. Within this ledger, a specific subset of $t$ accounts has been flagged for priority review.

You have a catalog of $m$ available reconciliation procedures. Each procedure involves exactly two distinct accounts and incurs a specific execution cost $w$.

Your objective is to select a subset of these procedures to execute. Let $C$ be your chosen collection of procedures. The collection $C$ must satisfy a strict regulatory property:
Imagine assigning each of the $n$ accounts to one of two theoretical tax jurisdictions: *Jurisdiction A* or *Jurisdiction B*. For every possible way to assign the $n$ accounts to these two jurisdictions, if the $t$ flagged accounts are not all assigned to the same jurisdiction, then your collection $C$ must contain at least one procedure that involves one account assigned to Jurisdiction A and one account assigned to Jurisdiction B.

You may select procedures that do not involve any flagged accounts if they are needed to satisfy this property.

You are given that $m = n - 1$. Furthermore, the ledger's architecture guarantees that if you were to select all $m$ available procedures, the property would hold for the entire ledger: for *any* assignment where the $n$ accounts do not all fall into the same jurisdiction, there is at least one procedure among the $m$ available that involves an account in Jurisdiction A and an account in Jurisdiction B.

Determine the minimum total execution cost required to choose a valid collection $C$ that satisfies the regulatory property for the $t$ flagged accounts.

## Input

The first line provides three integers **n**, **m**, and **t** (1 ≤ **t** ≤ **n**, 1 ≤ **n** ≤ 200000, **m** = **n** − 1).
The second line lists **t** distinct integers representing the IDs of the flagged accounts.
Following this are **m** lines, each containing three integers **u**, **v**, and **w** (1 ≤ **u**, **v** ≤ **n**, **u** ≠ **v**, 1 ≤ **w** ≤ 1000000). These values indicate an available reconciliation procedure between account **u** and account **v** that costs **w** to execute.

## Output

Output a single integer representing the minimum possible total cost to execute a collection of procedures that satisfies the regulatory property for the flagged accounts.

</STATEMENT>
<RATIONALE>
The problem was completely reframed from abstract sets and subsets to an accounting scenario involving tax jurisdictions. The subset intersection condition (which trivially maps to a graph cut) was replaced with a 2-coloring constraint where accounts are assigned to Jurisdiction A or B. Models can no longer rely on recognizing standard subset notation to trigger tree/graph connectivity algorithms; they must now independently deduce that preventing any 2-coloring from separating the flagged accounts is mathematically identical to finding the minimal connected subtree spanning those accounts.
</RATIONALE>
<PRESERVED_ANSWER>
The condition that any assignment splitting the $t$ accounts must have a selected procedure crossing the split is mathematically identical to requiring all $t$ accounts to be in the same connected component of the chosen subgraph, so the required set of edges and their minimum cost is exactly the same.
</PRESERVED_ANSWER>
<PRESERVED_CLASS>
The input is still a tree (guaranteed by $n$ nodes, $m=n-1$ edges, and the connectivity guarantee), and the problem still asks for the weight of the unique minimal subtree spanning $t$ nodes, which remains solvable in $O(n)$ time.
</PRESERVED_CLASS>
