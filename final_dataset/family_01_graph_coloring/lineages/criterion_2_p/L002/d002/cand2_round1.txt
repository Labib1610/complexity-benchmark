# Module 2: Clearance Allocation

Time limit: 1 s   Memory limit: 256 MB

The annual corporate audit runs in three sequential phases. Initially, the HR analytics engine reviews historical incident logs, identifying specific pairs of employees who require mutual oversight. 

You are building the second phase: the clearance allocation processor. This system processes the oversight pairs and assigns a security tier to each employee. The available tiers are `1` and `2`. To guarantee operational stability, any two employees $u$ and $v$ who require mutual oversight must have their tiers assigned such that the bitwise XOR of their tier values evaluates to exactly 3.

If no such tier configuration exists for the entire workforce, the processor must halt the audit. When multiple valid configurations exist, compliance protocols dictate selecting the one that produces the lexicographically smallest sequence of tier assignments (evaluating the sequence from employee 1 up to the total number of employees).

The processor's output is subsequently passed to the third phase, where the payroll system uses the tiers to calculate shift differentials.

## Input
The first line contains two integers. The first integer represents the total workforce headcount. The company guarantees there is at least one employee, while the legacy HR database caps the workforce at exactly two hundred thousand individuals. The second integer represents the number of oversight pairs, which can be as few as zero and is limited by the analytics engine's maximum capacity of two hundred thousand records.

The following lines each contain two integers $u$ and $v$, representing a pair of employees (identified by their 1-based index, from 1 to the total headcount) who require mutual oversight.

## Output
If the processor cannot generate a valid configuration, print `NO` on a single line.
Otherwise, print `YES` on the first line. On the second line, print a sequence of integers (each either `1` or `2`), representing the tier assigned to each employee in order from 1 to the total headcount. This sequence must be the lexicographically smallest valid configuration.

## Example 1
Input:
```
4 4
1 2
2 3
3 4
1 4
```
Output:
```
YES
1 2 1 2
```

## Example 2
Input:
```
3 3
1 2
1 3
2 3
```
Output:
```
NO
```

<RATIONALE>
The affordance that made bipartiteness obvious was the condition that the product of the two levels (which are 1 and 2) must be exactly 2. I replaced this with the condition that the bitwise XOR of the two levels must be exactly 3. Since the only available values are 1 and 2, this mathematically enforces that the two values must be different, forcing models to deduce the inequality constraint rather than pattern-matching the product rule. I also implemented Strategy S07 by describing the numeric bounds as scenario constraints (database caps and capacities).
</RATIONALE>
<PRESERVED_ANSWER>
The bitwise XOR of any two values chosen from {1, 2} is 3 if and only if one value is 1 and the other is 2 (since 1^1=0, 2^2=0, and 1^2=3), so the set of valid configurations and the lexicographical requirement remain mathematically identical to the original product condition.
</PRESERVED_ANSWER>
