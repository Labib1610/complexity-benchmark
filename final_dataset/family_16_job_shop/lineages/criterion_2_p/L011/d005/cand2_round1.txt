# Problem B — Magnetic Suspension Tower

Time limit: 1 s   Memory limit: 256 MB

## Description

You are tasked with stacking **j** modular containers to form a single vertical tower. The tower will have exactly **j** levels, with level 1 at the highest point (the top) and level **j** at the base (the bottom).

Each container has two distinct mass properties:
- A structural mass, $p_1$
- A payload mass, $p_2$

The fully assembled tower is held in place by a specialized magnetic suspension system. Due to the interaction between gravity and the magnetic field, each container experiences a specific amount of internal stress. 

The internal stress on the container located at level $k$ (where $1 \le k \le j$) is exactly the sum of:
1. The downward gravitational force, which equals the sum of the structural masses ($p_1$) of all containers from level 1 down to level $k$ (inclusive).
2. The upward magnetic force, which equals the sum of the payload masses ($p_2$) of all containers from level $k$ down to level **j** (inclusive).

In other words, the container at level $k$ is squeezed between the structural mass of itself and everything above it, and the payload mass of itself and everything below it.

Your objective is to determine an ordering of the **j** containers—assigning each to a unique level from 1 to **j**—that minimizes the maximum internal stress experienced by any single container in the tower.

What is the minimum possible value for this maximum internal stress?

## Input

The first line contains a single integer **j** (1 ≤ j ≤ 200000).
Each of the following **j** lines contains two integers $p_1$ and $p_2$ (1 ≤ $p_1, p_2$ ≤ 1000000), representing the structural mass and the payload mass of a container, respectively.

## Output

Print a single integer: the minimum possible value of the maximum internal stress.

## Examples

### Example 1

**Input**
```
3
3 2
2 4
1 3
```

**Output**
```
10
```

### Example 2

**Input**
```
1
5 3
```

**Output**
```
8
```

### Example 3

**Input**
```
3
2 2
2 2
2 2
```

**Output**
```
8
```

## Note

In Example 1, let the containers be $C_1$ (3, 2), $C_2$ (2, 4), and $C_3$ (1, 3).
An optimal arrangement places $C_3$ at level 1, $C_2$ at level 2, and $C_1$ at level 3.
- **Level 1 ($C_3$):** Downward force is the structural mass of $C_3$ (1). Upward force is the payload mass of $C_3, C_2, C_1$ (3 + 4 + 2 = 9). Stress = 1 + 9 = 10.
- **Level 2 ($C_2$):** Downward force is the structural mass of $C_3, C_2$ (1 + 2 = 3). Upward force is the payload mass of $C_2, C_1$ (4 + 2 = 6). Stress = 3 + 6 = 9.
- **Level 3 ($C_1$):** Downward force is the structural mass of $C_3, C_2, C_1$ (1 + 2 + 3 = 6). Upward force is the payload mass of $C_1$ (2). Stress = 6 + 2 = 8.

The maximum internal stress across all levels is $\max(10, 9, 8) = 10$.

<RATIONALE>
The problem was completely remapped from a sequential time-based scheduling task (two-machine flow-shop) into a static structural engineering task (minimizing peak stress in a vertical stack). This eliminates all affordances related to "Main Stage", "Review Room", and identical processing orders. To solve it, a model can no longer rely on pattern-matching the scenario to Johnson's rule; it must now formally derive the adjacent pairwise exchange condition for the prefix/suffix sum formula, which proves genuine algorithmic reasoning.
</RATIONALE>
<PRESERVED_ANSWER>
The makespan of a two-machine flow-shop is mathematically identical to the maximum over all k of the sum of the first machine's processing times up to k and the second machine's processing times from k to j, so the optimal value remains exactly the same for all inputs.
</PRESERVED_ANSWER>
