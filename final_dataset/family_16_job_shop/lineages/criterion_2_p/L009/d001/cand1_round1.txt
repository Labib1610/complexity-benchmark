# Problem B — Visual Effects Pipeline

Time limit: 1 s   Memory limit: 256 MB

## Description

A studio is rendering **j** visual effects to form a continuous reel. Each effect consists of two components: a geometry sequence and a lighting sequence. For each effect, you are given the duration of its geometry sequence (**p1**) and the duration of its lighting sequence (**p2**).

You must choose the order in which these **j** effects are arranged in the reel. 

A quality control reviewer will watch this reel in a specific way. They will choose a switch point **k** (where 1 ≤ **k** ≤ **j**). They will watch the geometry sequences of the effects from position 1 through position **k**, and then immediately switch to watching the lighting sequences of the effects from position **k** through position **j**. 

The total time the reviewer spends watching is the sum of the geometry durations of the effects from position 1 to **k**, plus the sum of the lighting durations of the effects from position **k** to **j**. Note that for the effect at the switch point **k**, the reviewer watches both its geometry and its lighting sequences.

The reviewer will maliciously choose the switch point **k** that makes their total watching time as large as possible. 

Your task is to choose the arrangement of the **j** effects to minimize this maximum possible watching time. Report this smallest possible maximum watching time.

## Input

The first line contains a single integer **j** (1 ≤ **j** ≤ 200000).
Each of the next **j** lines contains two integers **p1** and **p2** (1 ≤ **p1**, **p2** ≤ 1000000): the geometry duration and the lighting duration of an effect.

## Output

Print a single integer: the smallest possible maximum watching time that the reviewer can be restricted to.

## Examples

### Example 1

**Input**
```
3
3 2
2 4
1 3
```

**Output**
```
10
```

### Example 2

**Input**
```
1
5 3
```

**Output**
```
8
```

### Example 3

**Input**
```
3
2 2
2 2
2 2
```

**Output**
```
8
```

## Note

In Example 1, if you arrange the effects in the order of the 3rd input effect, then the 2nd, and finally the 1st, their durations are (1, 3), (2, 4), and (3, 2). 
- If the reviewer switches at **k** = 1, they watch the 1st geometry (1) and the 1st, 2nd, and 3rd lighting (3+4+2), totaling 10.
- If they switch at **k** = 2, they watch the 1st and 2nd geometry (1+2) and the 2nd and 3rd lighting (4+2), totaling 9.
- If they switch at **k** = 3, they watch the 1st, 2nd, and 3rd geometry (1+2+3) and the 3rd lighting (2), totaling 8.
The reviewer will choose **k** = 1 for a total time of 10. No other arrangement of the effects can force the reviewer's maximum time to be less than 10.

In Example 2, there is only one effect. The reviewer must switch at **k** = 1, watching its geometry (5) and its lighting (3), totaling 8.
</STATEMENT>
<RATIONALE>
Replaced the explicit two-machine scheduling scenario with its exact algebraic equivalent, framed as a reviewer choosing a switch point to maximize their continuous watching time. This removes the canonical flow-shop vocabulary ("processor", "sequence", "finished first"), forcing models to either algebraically derive the sorting comparator via exchange arguments or independently recognize the structural equivalence to the two-machine makespan problem.
</RATIONALE>
<PRESERVED_ANSWER>
The maximum watching time for a chosen switch point exactly calculates the makespan of a two-machine permutation flow-shop for that sequence, so the optimal minimum time is mathematically identical.
</PRESERVED_ANSWER>
<PRESERVED_CLASS>
The problem remains exactly equivalent to the two-machine flow-shop scheduling problem, which is optimally solved in O(n log n) time using Johnson's rule.
</PRESERVED_CLASS>
<RISK>
Models might misinterpret whether the effect at switch point $k$ has both its geometry and lighting watched, but the description and the worked example explicitly clarify that both are included in the sum.
</RISK>
