<METHOD_DESCRIPTION>
Replaces the distinctness constraint (c_u != c_v) with the mathematically equivalent condition that their product must not equal the square of either assigned value. This avoids standard graph-coloring terminology and the forbidden word "polynomial" while remaining completely rigorous, forcing solvers to evaluate the algebraic condition rather than pattern-matching to 3-coloring.
</METHOD_DESCRIPTION>

<STATEMENT>
# Problem A — Museum Curation

Time limit: 2 s   Memory limit: 256 MB

You are organizing a highly anticipated exhibition featuring $n$ interactive installations. The venue supports exactly three distinct frequency multipliers for the installations' audio elements, designated as 1, 2, and 3.

Every installation $i$ must be assigned exactly one frequency multiplier $c_i \in \{1, 2, 3\}$. However, certain pairs of installations interact. You have compiled a list of $m$ interacting pairs. For any such pair $(u, v)$, their combined acoustic profile is evaluated by taking the product of their assigned frequency multipliers. 

To ensure no acoustic interference occurs, this product must never equal the square of the multiplier assigned to installation $u$, nor can it equal the square of the multiplier assigned to installation $v$. If the product equals the square of either installation's multiplier, it creates an unstable resonance loop and the assignment is invalid.

You must determine whether it is possible to assign a frequency multiplier to all $n$ installations such that this interference condition is strictly avoided for all given pairs.

The venue has exactly forty available exhibit spaces, meaning the total number of installations $n$ will never exceed this quantity, while containing at least one. The list of interacting pairs may be completely empty, or it may contain up to the theoretical maximum where every installation interacts with every other installation.

## Input
The first line contains two integers $n$ and $m$ — the number of installations and the number of interacting pairs.
Each of the subsequent $m$ lines contains two integers $u$ and $v$, representing an interacting pair of installations. Installations are indexed from 1 to $n$.

## Output
If no such assignment is possible, output NO on a single line.
Otherwise, output YES on the first line. On the second line, output $n$ integers $c_1 \ c_2 \dots c_n$ (each being 1, 2, or 3), where $c_i$ is the frequency multiplier assigned to installation $i$. If multiple valid assignments exist, you may output any of them.

## Example 1
Input:
```
4 5
1 2
1 3
2 3
1 4
2 4
```
Output:
```
YES
1 2 3 3
```

## Example 2
Input:
```
4 6
1 2
1 3
1 4
2 3
2 4
3 4
```
Output:
```
NO
```

<RATIONALE>
I replaced the perfect square condition with the requirement that the product of the multipliers of a pair must not equal the square of either multiplier. This is mathematically equivalent to requiring distinct assignments without using any complexity-class vocabulary or structural giveaways. I also explicitly hid the numeric bounds within the text using strategy S07 to prevent surface-level pattern matching based on typical graph sizes.
</RATIONALE>
<PRESERVED_ANSWER>
Since the multipliers are strictly positive, the condition $c_u \times c_v \neq c_u^2$ simplifies exactly to $c_u \neq c_v$, which is logically identical to the original perfect square condition for the domain {1, 2, 3}, ensuring all valid outputs remain exactly the same.
</PRESERVED_ANSWER>
<PRESERVED_CLASS>
The problem remains identical to 3-COLORING, which is NP-complete, as the core constraint still enforces that adjacent vertices in a graph must receive different colors from a palette of size 3.
</PRESERVED_CLASS>
<RISK>
The phrase "theoretical maximum where every installation interacts with every other installation" might be seen as slightly wordy for the upper bound of $m$, though it unambiguously implies $n(n-1)/2$.
</RISK>
