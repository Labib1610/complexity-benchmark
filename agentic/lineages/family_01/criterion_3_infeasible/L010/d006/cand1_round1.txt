<METHOD_DESCRIPTION>Replaces the parity/degree-based definition of a triangle with a set-theoretic equivalent: three edges form a triangle if and only if the symmetric difference of any two edges equals the third edge. This hides the graph structure entirely, preventing models from recognizing it as a triangle-finding problem and using standard adjacency intersections.</METHOD_DESCRIPTION>

<STATEMENT>
# Problem C — Proof of Impossibility

Time limit: 2 s   Memory limit: 256 MB

The factory management has a log of incompatibility reports. Each report identifies a pair of workers who cannot share a shift. To prove that their current two-shift system is doomed to fail, management wants to find a specific pattern in the log. Your job is not to schedule the workers, but to search the log to certify this impossibility.

A "deadlock" is formed by a selection of exactly three distinct reports from the log that satisfies a strict requirement: if you take any two of these three reports, the workers who are named in exactly one of those two reports must be precisely the two workers named in the third report. Finding this configuration provides an undeniable certificate that two shifts cannot suffice.

It is guaranteed that the log contains at least one such deadlock. You must output the IDs of the workers who appear in the chosen reports of the deadlock.

## Input
The first line contains two integers $n$ and $m$ ($3 \le n \le 2000$, $3 \le m \le \min(n \cdot (n-1)/2, 2 \cdot 10^5)$).
Each of the next $m$ lines contains two integers $u$ and $v$ — a report naming two incompatible workers. All reports in the log represent distinct pairs.
It is guaranteed that at least one deadlock exists in the given reports.

## Output
Print the IDs of the distinct workers who appear in the deadlock's reports on one line, sorted in ascending order. If there are multiple valid deadlocks, output the one that produces the lexicographically smallest sorted sequence of worker IDs.

## Example 1
### Input
4 5
1 2
1 3
2 3
2 4
3 4
### Output
1 2 3

## Example 2
### Input
5 6
1 2
1 4
2 4
3 4
3 5
4 5
### Output
1 2 4
</STATEMENT>
<RATIONALE>
I removed the parenthetical that explicitly stated the subset reduces to exactly three distinct workers, which the remaining models used to directly map the problem to triangle-finding. Instead, I redefined the deadlock using a set-theoretic symmetric difference condition: the workers in any one report must be exactly those appearing in only one of the other two reports. This forces models to independently prove that this condition on pairs is mathematically equivalent to a 3-cycle before they can safely use an efficient graph algorithm.
</RATIONALE>
<PRESERVED_ANSWER>
The symmetric difference of any two distinct pairs of elements equals a third pair if and only if the three pairs mutually share exactly one element, forming a triangle; therefore, the valid deadlocks and their resulting worker sets are identical to those in the seed problem.
</PRESERVED_ANSWER>
<PRESERVED_CLASS>
The problem remains exactly the task of finding a triangle (K3) in a graph to certify non-2-colorability, which is solvable in $O(m \sqrt{m})$ or $O(nm/64)$ time, keeping it in the same complexity class.
</PRESERVED_CLASS>
<RISK>
Models might misinterpret "workers who are named in exactly one of those two reports" if they fail to recognize it as the symmetric difference of the two sets, though the phrasing is mathematically precise and unambiguous.
</RISK>
