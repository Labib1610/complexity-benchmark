# Problem C — Linguistic Panels

Time limit: 2 s   Memory limit: 256 MB

You are organizing an international linguistic conference featuring distinct languages, numbered 1 through $n$. To facilitate communication, you have hired bilingual interpreters. Each interpreter is certified to translate between a specific combination of two languages. 

The conference organizers want to host a special "closed discussion panel." A valid panel is formed by selecting exactly three interpreters from your roster such that their combined certifications involve exactly three distinct languages in total. 

Given the roster of hired interpreters, your task is to identify the three languages that make up such a panel.

## Input
The first line provides two integers $n$ and $m$. The conference facility limits the number of supported languages $n$ to a maximum of 2000, while a minimum of 3 is required for the event. The interpreter roster size $m$ has a minimum of 3 and a maximum headcount of 200,000, though it will never exceed the total number of unique two-language combinations possible among the supported languages.

The following $m$ lines each contain two integers $u$ and $v$ — representing an interpreter in your roster who translates between language $u$ and language $v$.
There is at most one interpreter for any given combination of two languages.
It is guaranteed that at least one valid closed discussion panel can be formed.

## Output
Output three integers $a$ $b$ $c$ on a single line, ordered such that $a < b < c$, representing the languages in the panel. If multiple valid sets of three languages exist, output the one that is lexicographically smallest (the one with the smallest $a$; in case of a tie, the smallest $b$; and finally the smallest $c$).

## Example 1
Input:
4 5
1 2
1 3
2 3
2 4
3 4

Output:
1 2 3

## Example 2
Input:
5 6
1 2
1 4
2 4
3 4
3 5
4 5

Output:
1 2 4

<RATIONALE>
The explicit bounds were rewritten as logistical constraints (facility capacity and headcount) to force the solver to derive the numeric inequalities from the scenario. The explicit description of finding three languages and checking all three pairs was replaced with the mathematically equivalent but structurally different condition of finding three edges (interpreters) that collectively span exactly three vertices (languages), forcing the model to deduce that this implies a 3-cycle.
</RATIONALE>
<PRESERVED_ANSWER>
The requirement of finding 3 edges spanning exactly 3 vertices in a simple graph is strictly equivalent to finding a triangle, so the valid panels and their lexicographical ordering remain identical.
</PRESERVED_ANSWER>
<PRESERVED_CLASS>
The underlying problem remains finding a 3-cycle in an undirected graph, which has the same computational complexity (solvable by bitset intersection) and remains in the same complexity class.
</PRESERVED_CLASS>
<RISK>
Models might misinterpret "combined certifications involve exactly three distinct languages" as allowing one interpreter to translate between three languages, though the prior sentence defines certifications as strictly between two languages.
</RISK>
