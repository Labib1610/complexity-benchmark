# Problem C — Proof of Impossibility

Time limit: 2 s   Memory limit: 256 MB

The laboratory still has only two storage units, 1 and 2, but the allocation is known to be hopeless. Your job is not to assign units — it is to certify the impossibility. It is guaranteed that the registry contains at least one group of three chemicals where each chemical in the group is reactive with both of the other two. Any such group proves two units cannot suffice. Output one such group.

## Input
The first line contains two integers $n$ and $m$. The number of chemicals $n$ is at least the number of chemicals in the required group, and is constrained by the laboratory's maximum capacity of two thousand chemicals. The number of reactive pairs $m$ is at least three. The registry's physical ledger can hold up to two hundred thousand entries, so $m$ will not exceed this limit, nor will it exceed the theoretical maximum number of distinct pairs that can be formed from $n$ chemicals.

Each of the next $m$ lines contains two integers $u$ $v$ — a reactive pair.
It is guaranteed that at least one such group of three exists.

## Output
Print three integers $a$ $b$ $c$ on one line with $a < b < c$ such that $a$ is reactive with $b$ and $c$, and $b$ is reactive with $c$. Output the lexicographically smallest such group (smallest $a$; ties broken by smallest $b$; then smallest $c$).

## Example 1
Input:
```
4 5
1 2
1 3
2 3
2 4
3 4
```
Output:
```
1 2 3
```

## Example 2
Input:
```
5 6
1 2
1 4
2 4
3 4
3 5
4 5
```
Output:
```
1 2 4
```

The phrase "pairwise react" strongly triggers graph-triangle detection templates in models. This was replaced with "each chemical in the group is reactive with both of the other two", forcing the model to reconstruct the logical structure of a 3-clique. The numeric bounds were also removed from standard mathematical notation and embedded into the scenario's physical limits.
</RATIONALE>
