<METHOD_NAME>Pigeonhole Constraint Framing</METHOD_NAME>
<METHOD_DESCRIPTION>Replaces the explicit algebraic zero-energy condition with a 2-coloring constraint on 3 vertices. Since 3 vertices must be assigned to 2 colors, by the Pigeonhole Principle at least two must share a color. The only way this always forces an edge conflict is if all three vertices are mutually connected (a triangle). Models that take the text literally will write an O(n^3) search and TLE, while models that reason logically will realize it's just finding a triangle and use the fast O(n*m/64) bitset approach.</METHOD_DESCRIPTION>

<STATEMENT>
# Critical Interference

Time limit: 2 s
Memory limit: 256 MB

A proposed communications standard attempts to allocate $n$ transceivers (labeled 1 through $n$) into a network. However, testing has revealed $m$ specific bidirectional interference links between pairs of transceivers. Each link connects two distinct transceivers, and no two links connect the same pair.

To diagnose the core issue in the network's topology, engineers analyze subsets of exactly three transceivers. 
A triplet of transceivers is considered *critically flawed* if it is impossible to assign each of the three transceivers to one of two available operating channels such that no two transceivers on the same channel share an interference link.

Your objective is to isolate a subset of exactly three transceivers that is critically flawed. 

## Input
The first line contains the transceiver count $n$ and the interference link count $m$ ($3 \le n \le 2000$, $3 \le m \le \min(n \cdot (n-1)/2, 2 \cdot 10^5)$).
The following $m$ lines each detail a pair of transceivers $u$ and $v$ that share a mutual interference link.
At least one critically flawed subset of three transceivers is guaranteed to exist.

## Output
Provide the labels of the three transceivers forming the subset, printed on a single line as three integers $a$, $b$, and $c$ with $a < b < c$.
Among all valid triples $(a, b, c)$ with $a < b < c$, choose the one with the smallest $a$; if there is a tie, choose the one with the smallest $b$; if there is still a tie, choose the one with the smallest $c$.

## Example 1
### Input
```
4 5
1 2
1 3
2 3
2 4
3 4
```
### Output
```
1 2 3
```

## Example 2
### Input
```
5 6
1 2
1 4
2 4
3 4
3 5
4 5
```
### Output
```
1 2 4
```

To succeed efficiently, the model must deduce via the Pigeonhole Principle that a triplet cannot be divided into two conflict-free channels if and only if all three transceivers mutually interfere (a triangle), forcing logical reasoning rather than pattern matching the algebraic form.
</RATIONALE>
