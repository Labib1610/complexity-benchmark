<STATEMENT>
# Ternary Quantum Coherence

Time limit: 2 s
Memory limit: 256 MB

A physics laboratory is working with a system of $n$ trapped ions. The physical capacity of the ion trap supports a maximum of 2000 ions, and the experiment requires a minimum of 3. The scientists have performed a series of operations, successfully creating $m$ distinct two-particle entanglements. The laser system's storage limits the recorded entanglements to at most 200000 (or the theoretical maximum of exactly one entanglement per pair of ions, whichever is smaller), and at least 3 entanglements are always recorded. Each entanglement links a specific pair of ions.

The next phase of the experiment requires isolating a coherent ternary subsystem—a group of exactly three distinct ions. For the subsystem to maintain the required quantum coherence, it must not contain any missing links: it is strictly forbidden for any two ions within the chosen trio to lack a two-particle entanglement between them.

You are provided with the log of the $m$ successful two-particle entanglements. It is guaranteed that the system contains at least one valid ternary subsystem. Your task is to identify the three ions that form it.

## Input
The first line contains two integers $n$ and $m$, representing the number of trapped ions and the number of two-particle entanglements, respectively. The bounds for $n$ and $m$ strictly follow the physical and storage limits described above. The ions are assigned integer identifiers from $1$ to $n$.

Each of the following $m$ lines contains two integers $u$ and $v$ ($1 \le u, v \le n$, $u \neq v$), indicating that the ion $u$ and the ion $v$ share an entanglement.

It is guaranteed that at least one valid ternary subsystem can be identified.

## Output
Print three integers $a$, $b$, and $c$ on a single line, separated by spaces, representing the identifiers of the three ions that form the subsystem. The identifiers must be printed in strictly increasing order ($a < b < c$).

If multiple such subsystems exist, output the one that is lexicographically smallest (i.e., minimize $a$; in case of a tie, minimize $b$; and if still tied, minimize $c$).

## Example 1
Input:
```
4 5
1 2
1 3
2 3
2 4
3 4
```
Output:
```
1 2 3
```

## Example 2
Input:
```
5 6
1 2
1 4
2 4
3 4
3 5
4 5
```
Output:
```
1 2 4
```

<RATIONALE>
The problem statement was updated to express the numeric limits of the variables $n$ and $m$ through the physical constraints of the laboratory setting, rather than providing bare mathematical inequalities. Furthermore, the description of the required three-ion group was changed from a positive "completely interconnected" requirement to a negative constraint ("forbidden for any two ions... to lack a two-particle entanglement"). This forces the model to logically deduce that all pairs must be connected, rather than relying on surface-level keyword matching for a graph clique.
</RATIONALE>
<PRESERVED_ANSWER>
The requirement of finding exactly three ions where no two lack an entanglement is logically identical to finding three ions that are completely interconnected, so the same subsystem is selected and outputted in the same lexicographical order.
</PRESERVED_ANSWER>
