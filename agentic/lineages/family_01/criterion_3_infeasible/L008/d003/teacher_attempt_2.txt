<STATEMENT>
# Problem C — Ternary Reaction Hazard

Time limit: 2 s   Memory limit: 256 MB

You are analyzing a laboratory protocol involving a set of distinct chemical reagents. Through preliminary testing, you have a list of two-reagent combinations that produce a precipitate when mixed. 

A specific reaction hazard occurs among a trio of distinct reagents. To verify the hazard, a technician selects one reagent from the trio as the anchor. The anchor must be recorded in the testing data as precipitating with the second reagent, the anchor must be recorded as precipitating with the third reagent, and crucially, the second and third reagents must also be recorded as precipitating with each other. When these conditions are met, the trio is classified as a hazard. 

Your task is to identify one such hazard. The provided testing data guarantees that at least one hazardous trio exists among the reagents.

## Input
The first line provides two integers: the total inventory size and the number of logged precipitate records. 
The laboratory consists of 40 storage racks, each accommodating exactly 50 distinct reagents. The inventory size is at least 3 and cannot exceed the maximum combined capacity of these racks. 
The testing apparatus requires at least 3 logged pairs. Its log size cannot exceed two hundred thousand records or the maximum possible number of unique pairs for the given inventory size, whichever is smaller. 
Reagents are identified by integers from $1$ up to the inventory size.

The following lines, equal in number to the logged records, each contain two distinct integers $u$ and $v$ (between $1$ and the inventory size), indicating that reagent $u$ and reagent $v$ produce a precipitate when combined.

It is guaranteed that the provided records contain at least one hazardous trio.

## Output
Output three integers $a$, $b$, and $c$ on a single line, ordered such that $a < b < c$, representing the three reagents that form the hazard. If there is more than one such trio of reagents, you must output the one that minimizes the hazard score, defined as $a \cdot 10^8 + b \cdot 10^4 + c$.

## Example 1
Input:
```
4 5
1 2
1 3
2 3
2 4
3 4
```
Output:
```
1 2 3
```

## Example 2
Input:
```
5 6
1 2
1 4
2 4
3 4
3 5
4 5
```
Output:
```
1 2 4
```
</STATEMENT>
<RATIONALE>
The explicit definition of a triangle ("every possible two-reagent combination among those three is listed") was replaced with a procedural verification step involving an "anchor". This breaks the symmetry of the description, forcing the model to deduce that the anchor and its two partners mutually precipitating is logically equivalent to a 3-clique. The numeric bounds for $n$ and $m$ were also relocated into scenario-based indirect limits (e.g., 40 racks of 50 reagents for $n \le 2000$).
</RATIONALE>
<PRESERVED_ANSWER>
A trio verified by an anchor precipitating with two reagents that also precipitate with each other is exactly a set of three mutually precipitating reagents (a triangle), so the valid outputs and tie-breaking logic remain identical.
</PRESERVED_ANSWER>
<PRESERVED_CLASS>
The problem still requires finding a 3-clique in an undirected graph, preserving the $O(nm/64)$ bitset intersection complexity class.
</PRESERVED_CLASS>
<RISK>
A model might misinterpret the "anchor" as needing to be a specific sequence or ordered tuple in the output, but the output section explicitly asks for the trio ordered $a < b < c$, clarifying that the hazard is the un-ordered set of three reagents.
</RISK>
