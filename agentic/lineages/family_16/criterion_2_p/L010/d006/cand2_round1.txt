<METHOD_DESCRIPTION>Transforms a 2-machine concurrent scheduling problem into a 1-dimensional running-sum tracking problem. By defining a single accumulator (baseline heat) that starts at the sum of all machine-2 times and is updated by +machine1 -machine2, the peak value exactly mirrors the flow-shop makespan algebraically, but completely eliminates scheduling/job/machine semantics.</METHOD_DESCRIPTION>

<STATEMENT>
# Problem B — Thermal Peak Minimization

Time limit: 1 s   Memory limit: 256 MB

## Description

You are tasked with safely discharging $j$ energy cores in a sequence. You must choose an order $C_1, C_2, \dots, C_j$ (a permutation of the given cores) to process them one by one.

Each core has an activation energy $x$ and a cooling energy $y$. Let $x(C_k)$ and $y(C_k)$ denote the respective energies for the core placed at position $k$ in your sequence.

The processing reactor maintains a "baseline heat" level. Before the first core is processed, the reactor's baseline heat is initialized to the sum of the cooling energies of all $j$ cores:
$$\text{Initial Baseline Heat} = \sum_{i=1}^j y(C_i)$$

As you process the sequence of cores, the heat fluctuates. For each step $k$ from $1$ to $j$, the following two events occur in order:
1. **Heat Spike:** The core is activated, causing the reactor's heat to momentarily spike. The *peak heat* reached at step $k$ is exactly the current baseline heat plus the activation energy $x(C_k)$.
2. **Baseline Adjustment:** The core stabilizes, permanently altering the reactor's baseline heat. The new baseline heat becomes the old baseline heat, plus the activation energy $x(C_k)$, minus the cooling energy $y(C_k)$.

Your goal is to find a sequence of the $j$ cores that minimizes the maximum peak heat reached during any of the $j$ steps. Report this smallest possible maximum peak heat.

Note: The answer may exceed the range of a 32-bit signed integer.

## Input

The first line contains a single integer $j$ ($1 \le j \le 200000$).
Each of the next $j$ lines contains two integers $x$ and $y$ ($1 \le x, y \le 1000000$): the activation and cooling energies of a core.

## Output

Print a single integer: the smallest possible maximum peak heat achieved by an optimal ordering of the cores.

## Examples

### Example 1

**Input**
```
3
3 2
2 4
1 3
```

**Output**
```
10
```

### Example 2

**Input**
```
1
5 3
```

**Output**
```
8
```

### Example 3

**Input**
```
3
2 2
2 2
2 2
```

**Output**
```
8
```

## Note

In Example 1, consider the sequence of cores with energies (1, 3), (2, 4), and (3, 2).
The sum of all cooling energies is 3 + 4 + 2 = 9. The initial baseline heat is 9.
- **Step 1:** Core (1, 3) is processed.
  Peak heat = 9 (current baseline) + 1 (activation) = 10.
  New baseline heat = 9 + 1 - 3 = 7.
- **Step 2:** Core (2, 4) is processed.
  Peak heat = 7 (current baseline) + 2 (activation) = 9.
  New baseline heat = 7 + 2 - 4 = 5.
- **Step 3:** Core (3, 2) is processed.
  Peak heat = 5 (current baseline) + 3 (activation) = 8.
  New baseline heat = 5 + 3 - 2 = 6.

The maximum peak heat reached across all steps is $\max(10, 9, 8) = 10$. This is the minimum possible maximum peak heat for any arrangement of the three cores.

<RATIONALE>
The previous framing used two parallel tracks with segments, which models easily mapped to the two-machine flow-shop problem and Johnson's rule. This rewrite transforms the 2-stage concurrent scheduling problem into a 1-dimensional running-sum problem (baseline heat) with a specific initialization and delta. Models must now algebraically simplify the recurrence to discover it equals the flow-shop makespan equation before they can apply Johnson's rule, forcing genuine structural reasoning over surface pattern matching.
</RATIONALE>
<PRESERVED_ANSWER>
The peak heat at step $k$ algebraically simplifies to exactly $\sum_{i=1}^k x(C_i) + \sum_{i=k}^j y(C_i)$, which is the exact formula for the makespan of job $k$ in a 2-machine flow shop, meaning the optimal permutations and minimum maximums are mathematically identical to the seed.
</PRESERVED_ANSWER>
<PRESERVED_CLASS>
The problem remains solvable in O(N log N) time by sorting the elements using Johnson's rule, keeping it in the polynomial time complexity class P.
</PRESERVED_CLASS>
