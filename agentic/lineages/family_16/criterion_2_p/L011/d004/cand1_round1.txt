# Problem B — Bravery Test

Time limit: 1 s   Memory limit: 256 MB

## Description

You are designing a trial of bravery consisting of a $2 \times j$ grid of chambers. 

You have $j$ distinct trial segments to use. Each segment consists of an upper chamber containing $p_1$ hazards and a lower chamber containing $p_2$ hazards. You must assemble the trial by placing these $j$ segments in any left-to-right sequence you choose. This forms a grid with 2 rows and $j$ columns.

A challenger will enter the top-left chamber (the upper chamber of your first segment) and must navigate to the bottom-right chamber (the lower chamber of your last segment). From any chamber, the challenger can only move right (to the chamber in the same row of the next segment) or down (from the upper chamber to the lower chamber of the same segment). Moving left or up is not permitted. 

The challenger's "danger score" is the sum of the hazards in all the chambers they visit along their path. 

The challenger wants to prove their bravery, so they will evaluate your assembled trial and always choose a valid path that **maximizes** their danger score. 

As the designer, you want to ensure the trial is survivable. You must choose the left-to-right order of the $j$ segments to **minimize** the danger score of the challenger's chosen path. 

What is the lowest possible danger score the challenger can achieve, assuming you arrange the segments optimally and the challenger always finds the path with the maximum hazards in your arrangement?

## Input

The first line contains a single integer **j** (1 ≤ j ≤ 200000).
Each of the following **j** lines contains two integers **p1** and **p2** (1 ≤ p1, p2 ≤ 1000000), representing the number of hazards in the upper and lower chambers of that segment, respectively.

## Output

Print a single integer: the minimum possible danger score of the challenger's maximum-hazard path.

## Examples

### Example 1

**Input**
```
3
3 2
2 4
1 3
```

**Output**
```
10
```

### Example 2

**Input**
```
1
5 3
```

**Output**
```
8
```

### Example 3

**Input**
```
3
2 2
2 2
2 2
```

**Output**
```
8
```

## Note

In Example 1, if you arrange the segments in the order of the third input, then the second, then the first:
- The upper chambers have 1, 2, and 3 hazards.
- The lower chambers have 3, 4, and 2 hazards.

The challenger has three possible paths:
1. Move down in column 1: 1 (upper) + 3 (lower) + 4 (lower) + 2 (lower) = 10.
2. Move down in column 2: 1 (upper) + 2 (upper) + 4 (lower) + 2 (lower) = 9.
3. Move down in column 3: 1 (upper) + 2 (upper) + 3 (upper) + 2 (lower) = 8.

The challenger wants to maximize their score, so they choose the first path for a score of 10. No other arrangement of the segments results in a maximum path score lower than 10.

In Example 2, there is only one segment. The challenger visits the upper chamber (5 hazards) and then moves down to the lower chamber (3 hazards), accumulating 8 hazards.

<RATIONALE>
I reframed the scheduling problem into an isomorphic minimax grid path problem. The makespan of a 2-machine permutation flow shop is mathematically identical to the maximum path sum on a 2xj grid where the job times are the cell values. This removes all scheduling terminology, forcing the model to recognize the algebraic structure of the minimax path rather than pattern-matching "Main Stage" and "Review Room" to Johnson's rule.
</RATIONALE>
<PRESERVED_ANSWER>
The maximum path sum on a 2xj grid is exactly the formula for the makespan of a permutation schedule in a 2-machine flow shop, so the optimal arrangement yields the exact same minimum value.
</PRESERVED_ANSWER>
