<METHOD_NAME>Minimax Path Equivalence</METHOD_NAME>
<METHOD_DESCRIPTION>Transforms the 2-machine flow shop scheduling problem into a minimax grid-path problem (finding a permutation of columns to minimize the maximum path sum). This removes all scheduling vocabulary and the explicit dual-sequence flexibility, replacing them with a mathematical equivalent that models rely on surface cues to recognize.</METHOD_DESCRIPTION>

<STATEMENT>
# Problem B — Obstacle Course Design

Time limit: 1 s   Memory limit: 256 MB

## Description

You are designing a two-lane obstacle course using a set of modular segments. The total number of segments you must arrange is determined by the inventory of your storage facility, which holds up to two hundred thousand segments, and always contains at least one.

Each segment features two obstacles: one in the Upper Lane and one in the Lower Lane. You must arrange all the available segments in a single sequence from left to right. 

A competitor navigating the course begins at the first segment in the Upper Lane. They must choose exactly one segment at which to drop down to the Lower Lane. If they choose to drop at the $k$-th segment in your sequence, their route consists of:
- The Upper Lane obstacles of segments $1$ through $k$.
- The Lower Lane obstacles of segments $k$ through the end of the sequence.

The competitor's total time is simply the sum of the clearance times of all obstacles on their route. Since competitors are unpredictable, you must assume a competitor will happen to choose the drop point $k$ that results in the *maximum* possible total time for your chosen sequence.

Your task is to determine the optimal left-to-right order of the segments that minimizes this maximum possible total time.

## Input

The first line contains a single integer, representing the number of segments in your inventory. You will always have at least one segment, up to the warehouse's maximum capacity of 200,000 segments.
Each of the following lines corresponds to one segment and contains two integers. The first integer is the clearance time for its Upper Lane obstacle, and the second integer is the clearance time for its Lower Lane obstacle. Each clearance time is at least 1 millisecond, but a strict safety mechanism ensures no obstacle exceeds a time of 1,000,000 milliseconds.

## Output

Print a single integer: the minimum possible value of the maximum total time, assuming you order the segments optimally.

## Examples

### Example 1

**Input**
```
3
3 2
2 4
1 3
```

**Output**
```
10
```

### Example 2

**Input**
```
1
5 3
```

**Output**
```
8
```

### Example 3

**Input**
```
3
2 2
2 2
2 2
```

**Output**
```
8
```

## Note

In Example 2, there is only a single segment. The competitor must drop at segment 1. Their route includes the Upper Lane obstacle of segment 1 (5 milliseconds) and the Lower Lane obstacle of segment 1 (3 milliseconds). The total time is 8.
</STATEMENT>
<RATIONALE>
The original problem was framed as a two-machine flow-shop, which models rapidly recognized to apply Johnson's rule. I transformed the scenario into a minimax path-finding problem on a 2xN grid (an obstacle course where a runner drops from the upper to lower lane). This removes all scheduling terminology, forcing the model to mathematically prove the equivalence to a flow-shop in order to apply Johnson's rule.
</RATIONALE>
<PRESERVED_ANSWER>
The maximum path sum through a chosen sequence of segments exactly calculates the makespan of a permutation schedule in a 2-machine flow-shop, and since permutation schedules are always optimal for two machines, the minimum possible maximum time perfectly matches the minimum makespan of the original problem.
</PRESERVED_ANSWER>
<PRESERVED_CLASS>
The problem remains mathematically identical to a 2-machine flow-shop makespan minimization, which is solved in O(N log N) time using Johnson's rule, keeping it in the same complexity class (P).
</PRESERVED_CLASS>
