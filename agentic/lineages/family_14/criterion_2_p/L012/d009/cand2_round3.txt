<METHOD_NAME>Economic Equivalence Framing</METHOD_NAME>
<METHOD_DESCRIPTION>Shifts the domain from linear algebra (difference vectors spanning a subspace) to an economic market model where regulations establish links between commodities. By framing connectivity as "a sequence of regulations that links commodities" and avoiding all vector space or explicit tree graph terminology, models relying on pattern-matching vector subspaces or explicit graph traversals will struggle to map the problem correctly, forcing them to reason deeply about the required transitive relations.</METHOD_DESCRIPTION>

<STATEMENT>
# Commodity Exchange Regulation

Time limit: 2 s   Memory limit: 256 MB

## Description

You are analyzing a market of $n$ distinct commodities, indexed from 1 to $n$. A specific subset of $t$ commodities are designated as **Benchmark Commodities**.

There are $m$ proposed bilateral trade regulations available for implementation. Each regulation directly links two distinct commodities $u$ and $v$. Implementing a regulation into your active policy incurs a specific scalar administrative cost.

The complete set of $m$ available regulations contains exactly $m = n - 1$ proposals. Furthermore, if all $m$ available regulations were implemented, it would be possible to link any commodity in the market to any other commodity through a sequence of these regulations (where each regulation in the sequence shares a commodity with the next, forming a continuous chain).

You must select a subset of these proposed regulations to implement. Your selection must satisfy the following condition: for any pair of Benchmark Commodities $a$ and $b$, there must exist a sequence of regulations strictly within your selected subset that links $a$ and $b$ in this manner. (If the number of Benchmark Commodities is exactly one, this condition imposes no restrictions, as no pairs exist).

Your objective is to determine the minimum total administrative cost required to select a valid subset of regulations that meets this condition.

## Input

The first line contains three integers: $n$, $m$, and $t$.
- $n$ is the number of commodities. The market size is constrained by a minimum of one commodity and a maximum registry capacity of 200,000 entries.
- $m$ is the number of proposed regulations, which is exactly $n - 1$.
- $t$ is the number of Benchmark Commodities. This count is at least one and can be at most the full market size $n$.

The second line contains $t$ distinct integers: the indices of the Benchmark Commodities (from 1 to $n$).

Each of the next $m$ lines details an available proposed regulation with three integers: $u$, $v$, and $w$.
- $u$ and $v$ are the distinct commodity indices (from 1 to $n$) linked by the regulation.
- $w$ is the administrative cost to implement this regulation. This cost is a positive integer that cannot exceed a statutory limit of 1,000,000.

These $m$ lines form the exact structure described above, guaranteeing the full set of proposals can link all commodities.

## Output

Print a single integer: the minimum total administrative cost required to ensure all pairs of Benchmark Commodities are linked by sequences of the selected regulations.

## Examples

### Example 1

**Input**
```
4 3 2
1 4
1 2 5
2 3 1
3 4 2
```

**Output**
```
8
```

### Example 2

**Input**
```
5 4 1
3
1 2 4
2 3 6
3 4 2
4 5 9
```

**Output**
```
0
```

### Example 3

**Input**
```
3 2 3
1 2 3
1 2 4
2 3 5
```

**Output**
```
9
```

## Note

In Example 1, the Benchmark Commodities are 1 and 4. The commodities 1 and 4 can only be linked by implementing all three proposed regulations, since the continuous chain must link 1 to 2, 2 to 3, and 3 to 4. The minimum cost is therefore 5 + 1 + 2 = 8. 

In Example 2, there is only one Benchmark Commodity. Since no pairs of Benchmark Commodities exist, no regulations need to be implemented, resulting in a minimum cost of 0.
</STATEMENT>
<RATIONALE>
The problem was rewritten to use an economic regulatory framework instead of vector spaces. The algebraic requirement of "difference vectors spanning a target difference" was translated to "a sequence of regulations linking two benchmark commodities". This preserves the exact logical constraints (finding a Steiner tree on a given tree) while completely removing the vector space and explicit graph theory terminology that models were relying on to identify the problem's underlying tree structure.
</RATIONALE>
<PRESERVED_ANSWER>
The condition that a subset of regulations must link all pairs of benchmark commodities is mathematically identical to selecting a subset of difference vectors whose span includes the difference vectors between all target dimensions.
</PRESERVED_ANSWER>
<PRESERVED_CLASS>
The problem remains equivalent to finding the minimum weight Steiner tree on a graph that is guaranteed to be a tree, which is solvable in O(n) using a tree traversal, keeping it in the polynomial time class P.
</PRESERVED_CLASS>
<RISK>
The phrase "sequence of regulations... forming a continuous chain" could be interpreted as requiring a single simple path that visits all benchmark commodities at once, rather than a union of paths that mutually connect them, though the phrase "for any pair... there must exist a sequence" strongly implies pairwise connectivity.
</RISK>
