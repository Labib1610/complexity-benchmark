<STATEMENT>
# Corporate Consolidation

Time limit: 2 s   Memory limit: 256 MB

## Description

In a regulated market, there are $n$ independent corporations (numbered 1 through $n$) and a set of $m$ proposed bilateral merger agreements. Each agreement involves two specific corporations and requires a defined legal fee to finalize. Whenever an agreement is finalized, the two corporations—along with any affiliates they have previously merged with—consolidate into a single unified conglomerate.

To maintain market equilibrium, the antitrust regulatory board has issued a certificate that enforces strict structural limits on the proposed agreements. Initially, the $n$ corporations act as $n$ independent entities. The board has verified that if all $m$ proposed agreements were finalized, all $n$ corporations would merge into a single unified conglomerate. Furthermore, the certificate guarantees absolute efficiency across the proposals: no proposed agreement is redundant. Regardless of which subset of agreements has already been finalized, finalizing any unchosen proposed agreement will always merge two disconnected entities, strictly reducing the total number of independent entities in the market by one.

Within this market, a particular group of $t$ corporations are identified as key patent holders. Your goal is to choose a subset of the proposed agreements to finalize such that all $t$ key patent holders are ultimately absorbed into the same conglomerate, and the total legal fee incurred is minimized.

## Input

The first line contains three integers $n$, $m$, and $t$ ($1 \le t \le n$, $1 \le n \le 200000$).
The second line contains $t$ distinct integers: the identifiers of the key patent holders.
Each of the next $m$ lines contains three integers $u$, $v$, and $w$ ($1 \le u, v \le n$, $u \neq v$, $1 \le w \le 1000000$), representing a proposed agreement between corporation $u$ and corporation $v$ with a legal fee of $w$.

## Output

Print a single integer: the minimum total legal fee required to reach a market state where all key patent holders belong to the same conglomerate.

## Examples

### Example 1

**Input**
```
4 3 2
1 4
1 2 5
2 3 1
3 4 2
```

**Output**
```
8
```

### Example 2

**Input**
```
5 4 1
3
1 2 4
2 3 6
3 4 2
4 5 9
```

**Output**
```
0
```

### Example 3

**Input**
```
3 2 3
1 2 3
1 2 4
2 3 5
```

**Output**
```
9
```

## Note

In Example 1, the key patent holders 1 and 4 are brought into the same conglomerate by finalizing the agreements that merge 1 with 2, 2 with 3, and 3 with 4. The total legal fee is 5 + 1 + 2 = 8. 
In Example 2, there is only one key patent holder, so the required market state is already satisfied and no agreements need to be finalized.
</STATEMENT>
<RATIONALE>
Removed the explicit mathematical guarantees of $m = n - 1$ and the induced-edge count limit. Replaced them with an operational description of the market: starting with $n$ entities, finalizing all $m$ agreements results in 1 entity, and every agreement strictly reduces the entity count by one regardless of order. This forces the model to logically deduce that the graph is a tree ($n \to 1$ in exactly $m$ steps implies $m = n-1$ and no cycles) rather than relying on surface-level keyword matching.
</RATIONALE>
<PRESERVED_ANSWER>
The structural guarantees still perfectly define a tree (connected and acyclic), so the underlying graph and the optimal subset of edges to connect the key nodes remain exactly the same.
</PRESERVED_ANSWER>
<PRESERVED_CLASS>
The problem remains equivalent to finding the Steiner tree on a tree, which is solvable in $O(n)$ time by pruning leaves or using a postorder traversal.
</PRESERVED_CLASS>
<RISK>
Models might misinterpret "strictly reducing the total number of independent entities" as implying they must actively find a way to reduce the entities, rather than it being a structural property of the input graph, though the phrase "certificate guarantees" mitigates this.
</RISK>
