<STATEMENT>
# Corporate Consolidation

Time limit: 2 s   Memory limit: 256 MB

## Description

A market consists of $n$ independent corporations, numbered 1 to $n$. A specific subset of $t$ corporations are designated as patent holders. There are $m$ potential merger agreements that can be executed between certain pairs of corporations, each requiring a specific legal fee to finalize.

Your objective is to execute a subset of these agreements so that all $t$ patent-holding corporations end up absorbed into the exact same conglomerate. When a merger agreement between two corporations is executed, they (along with any other corporations they have already merged with) consolidate into a single unified entity. You must ensure the patent holders are consolidated together at the minimum possible total legal fee.

To ensure the market contains no redundant legal pathways, the regulators have certified the $m$ proposed agreements with a strict structural guarantee: if an auditor were to evaluate the $m$ proposals one by one in any arbitrary order, executing an agreement if and only if it bridges two corporations that do not yet belong to the same unified entity, the auditor would end up executing every single one of the $m$ proposals. Furthermore, executing all $m$ proposals is guaranteed to consolidate all $n$ corporations into a single unified market.

## Input

The first line contains three integers $n$, $m$, and $t$ ($1 \le t \le n$, $1 \le n \le 200000$).
The second line contains $t$ distinct integers: the identifiers of the patent-holding corporations.
Each of the next $m$ lines contains three integers $u$, $v$, and $w$ ($1 \le u, v \le n$, $u \neq v$, $1 \le w \le 1000000$), representing a potential merger agreement between corporation $u$ and corporation $v$ with a legal fee of $w$.

## Output

Print a single integer: the minimum total legal fee to ensure all patent holders belong to the same conglomerate.

## Examples

### Example 1

**Input**
```
4 3 2
1 4
1 2 5
2 3 1
3 4 2
```

**Output**
```
8
```

### Example 2

**Input**
```
5 4 1
3
1 2 4
2 3 6
3 4 2
4 5 9
```

**Output**
```
0
```

### Example 3

**Input**
```
3 2 3
1 2 3
1 2 4
2 3 5
```

**Output**
```
9
```

## Note

In Example 1, the patent holders 1 and 4 are consolidated by executing the agreements that merge 1 with 2, 2 with 3, and 3 with 4, for a total fee of 5 + 1 + 2 = 8. In Example 2, there is only one patent holder, so no agreements need to be executed.

<RATIONALE>
The explicit guarantees that $m = n - 1$ and the density limit (which trivially imply a tree/forest) were removed. Instead, the problem now guarantees that a greedy cycle-checking process (an auditor executing mergers only if they don't form a cycle) would successfully execute all $m$ proposals without rejecting any, and that the result is fully connected. This forces the model to reason about the operational behavior of spanning-forest construction to deduce that the graph is acyclic and connected (a tree), rather than relying on surface pattern-matching of $m = n - 1$ or subset density rules.
</RATIONALE>
<PRESERVED_ANSWER>
The objective (minimum cost to connect a subset of nodes) and the underlying graph topology (a tree) remain exactly the same, so the unique minimal connecting subtree and its total cost are unchanged.
</PRESERVED_ANSWER>
<PRESERVED_CLASS>
The operational guarantee mathematically ensures the graph is connected and contains no cycles, meaning the input is strictly a tree, keeping the Steiner tree problem on this graph in P.
</PRESERVED_CLASS>
