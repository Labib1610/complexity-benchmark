<STATEMENT>
# Problem A — Compound Synthesis

Time limit: 5 s   Memory limit: 256 MB

## Description

You are an alchemist working with **n** elementary substances, numbered 1 through **n**. Initially, each substance is isolated in its own separate vial.

You have a manual containing **m** documented synthesis procedures. Each procedure specifies two elementary substances, **u** and **v**, and an energy cost **w**. 

Executing a procedure is a discrete physical event: you locate the vessel currently holding substance **u** and the vessel currently holding substance **v**. You pour their entire contents together into a new reaction vessel. The old vessels are discarded. The new vessel now holds every substance that was present in the two source vessels. The energy consumed by executing this specific procedure is exactly **w**. This cost is fixed by the procedure's recipe (which specifies **u** and **v**) and is completely unaffected by whatever other substances happen to be in the source vessels. (If **u** and **v** already share a vessel, the procedure cannot be executed).

You have been commissioned to run a sequence of procedures to isolate a specific set of **t** critical substances: $c_1, c_2, \dots, c_t$. Your commission is fulfilled the moment no two critical substances reside in different vessels. You may execute any sequence of the available procedures, potentially incorporating non-critical substances if they serve as cost-effective intermediate catalysts to bring the critical substances together.

Determine the minimum total energy required to fulfill this commission. (If $t=1$, the commission is fulfilled immediately and the minimum energy is 0).

## Input

The first line contains three integers **n**, **m**, and **t**. 
The laboratory's strict containment protocol limits the number of critical substances **t** to at least 1 and at most the number of sides of a decagon. 
The facility can track a total number of substances **n**, which is at least 1 and at most the square of the maximum allowed critical substances. 
The archive of documented procedures **m** contains at least 0 and no more than the theoretical maximum number of distinct two-substance combinations.

The second line contains **t** distinct integers $c_1, c_2, \dots, c_t$: the identifiers of the critical substances. The order of these identifiers does not matter.

Each of the next **m** lines contains three integers **u**, **v**, and **w** ($1 \le u, v \le n$, $u \neq v$). This indicates a documented procedure requiring substance **u** and substance **v**, consuming **w** energy. The energy **w** is a positive integer capped at one million joules.

It is guaranteed that the available procedures are sufficient to fulfill the commission.

## Output

Print a single integer: the minimum total energy required to reach a state where all critical substances share the same vessel.

## Examples

### Example 1

**Input**
```
4 6 3
1 2 3
1 4 1
2 4 1
3 4 1
1 2 2
2 3 2
1 3 2
```

**Output**
```
3
```

### Example 2

**Input**
```
4 4 3
1 2 3
1 2 1
2 3 1
1 4 10
3 4 10
```

**Output**
```
2
```

### Example 3

**Input**
```
3 2 1
1
1 2 5
2 3 5
```

**Output**
```
0
```

## Note

In Example 1, the critical substances are 1, 2, and 3. You can execute the procedures for (1,4), (2,4), and (3,4).
Executing (1,4) pours the vials of 1 and 4 into a new vessel (energy 1). This vessel holds {1, 4}.
Executing (2,4) pours the vial of 2 and the vessel holding {1, 4} into a new vessel (energy 1). This vessel holds {1, 2, 4}.
Executing (3,4) pours the vial of 3 and the vessel holding {1, 2, 4} into a new vessel (energy 1). This vessel holds {1, 2, 3, 4}.
At this point, critical substances 1, 2, and 3 all reside in the same vessel, so no two reside in different vessels. The total energy is 1 + 1 + 1 = 3.
Alternatively, executing (1,2) and (2,3) directly would consume 2 + 2 = 4 energy. The minimum energy is 3.

In Example 3, there is only one critical substance ($t=1$), so it is impossible for two to reside in different vessels. The commission is fulfilled immediately requiring 0 energy.
</STATEMENT>
<RATIONALE>
I shifted the framing from establishing equivalences between variables (which strongly suggests graph connectivity and connected components) to a physical sequence of merging chemical vessels. By describing the merging of components as discrete physical events ("pouring contents together") with fixed energy costs, the problem masquerades as a state-space simulation or a dynamic programming problem over sets (like an optimal merge pattern). Models relying on surface pattern matching will likely fail to extract the static graph structure required to recognize this as the Steiner Tree problem and apply the necessary Dreyfus-Wagner algorithm. Additionally, all numeric bounds in the input section are now derived indirectly through physical/geometric descriptions, forcing the model to deduce the limits before it can size its data structures.
</RATIONALE>
<PRESERVED_ANSWER>
The cost of executing a procedure is fixed and independent of vessel size, making the operations mathematically identical to selecting edges in a graph to form a connected component containing all critical vertices, so the minimum total energy exactly equals the minimum cost of the original equivalences.
</PRESERVED_ANSWER>
<PRESERVED_CLASS>
The problem still requires finding a minimum-weight connected subgraph that spans a specific subset of $t$ nodes in an undirected graph, which is precisely the NP-complete Steiner Tree problem.
</PRESERVED_CLASS>
