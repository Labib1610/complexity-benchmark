<METHOD_DESCRIPTION>Replaces the graph-connectivity requirement with the mathematically equivalent requirement of generating specific transpositions in the symmetric group. A set of available swaps can generate a transposition (i, j) if and only if i and j are in the same connected component of the swap graph. This hides the Steiner Tree structure entirely, defeating models that rely on surface pattern matching (like "odd parity" -> "paths") by forcing them to independently rediscover the equivalence between transposition generation and graph connectivity.</METHOD_DESCRIPTION>

<STATEMENT>
# Problem A — Cargo Reshuffling Optimization

Time limit: 5 s   Memory limit: 256 MB

## Description

You are managing a warehouse with **n** storage bays. Each bay currently holds exactly one distinct cargo container. 

You can purchase automated transfer tracks. Each track is hardwired between exactly two distinct bays, $u$ and $v$. When activated, the track exchanges the container currently in bay $u$ with the container currently in bay $v$. 

Once a track is purchased, it is permanently unlocked. You may activate your purchased tracks in any sequence, and you may activate the same track multiple times. 

A specific subset of **t** bays are designated as *high-priority*. Your task is to purchase a subset of available tracks such that the following condition is satisfied: 
For *every* pair of distinct high-priority bays $i$ and $j$, there must exist some sequence of activations of your purchased tracks that transitions the warehouse from its initial state to a target state where the container originally in bay $i$ and the container originally in bay $j$ have swapped places, while the containers originally in all other $n-2$ bays (both high-priority and standard) end up exactly in their original starting bays. 

The specific sequence of activations may be different for each pair of high-priority bays, but every sequence may only use tracks from the subset you purchased.

You are given a list of **m** available tracks, each with a one-time purchase cost **w**. Determine the minimum total cost of a subset of tracks that satisfies this requirement.

## Input

The first line provides three integers **n**, **m**, and **t**. 
The warehouse's priority zone has a strict capacity limit: the number of high-priority bays **t** can be any positive integer up to the number of prime numbers strictly less than 30.
The total number of bays **n** is physically constrained to be at least 1 and at most the square of the priority zone's maximum capacity.
The number of proposed tracks **m** is at least 0 and cannot exceed the theoretical maximum where every pair of bays has a dedicated track.

The second line lists **t** distinct integers representing the indices of the high-priority bays (1-indexed).
The following **m** lines each contain three integers **u**, **v**, and **w** (1 ≤ u, v ≤ n, u ≠ v). These represent an available track between distinct bays **u** and **v** that can be purchased for a cost of **w**. The cost **w** is a positive integer capped at the cube of one hundred.
It is guaranteed that it is possible to satisfy the requirement using the available tracks.

## Output

Output a single integer representing the minimum total cost of a subset of tracks that allows reaching the required target states for all pairs of high-priority bays.

## Note

In Example 1, the high-priority bays are 1, 2, and 3. We must be able to achieve three specific permutations from the initial state:
- Swap the contents of bays 1 and 2, leaving the contents of 3 and 4 in their original bays.
- Swap the contents of bays 2 and 3, leaving the contents of 1 and 4 in their original bays.
- Swap the contents of bays 1 and 3, leaving the contents of 2 and 4 in their original bays.

By purchasing the three tracks wired to (1, 4), (2, 4), and (3, 4) for a total cost of 1+1+1 = 3, we can satisfy the requirement. For instance, to swap the contents of 1 and 2 while leaving 3 and 4 unchanged, we can activate the tracks in this sequence: (1, 4), then (2, 4), then (1, 4). 
Let the initial containers be A, B, C, D in bays 1, 2, 3, 4. 
- Activate (1, 4): bays 1, 2, 3, 4 now hold D, B, C, A.
- Activate (2, 4): bays 1, 2, 3, 4 now hold D, A, C, B.
- Activate (1, 4): bays 1, 2, 3, 4 now hold B, A, C, D.
The containers originally in bays 1 and 2 are swapped, and the containers originally in 3 and 4 are back in their original bays. This is cheaper than purchasing the tracks (1, 2) and (2, 3) directly, which would cost 2+2=4.

In Example 3, there is only one high-priority bay. Because there are no pairs of distinct high-priority bays, the requirement is vacuously satisfied. No tracks need to be purchased, resulting in a cost of 0.

<RATIONALE>
The problem was reframed using permutation group theory. Generating a transposition (swapping exactly two items while leaving everything else in place) using a set of available swaps is mathematically equivalent to finding a path between those two items in the graph formed by the swaps. The models can no longer rely on the "odd parity" shortcut to recognize graph connectivity and must instead deduce that the permutation requirement strictly enforces a connected component containing all high-priority bays. Additionally, numeric bounds were replaced with physical/mathematical constraints (e.g., primes under 30) to meet the S07 strategy.
</RATIONALE>
<PRESERVED_ANSWER>
The condition that a subset of transpositions generates all transpositions among a specific set of elements is satisfied if and only if those elements lie in the same connected component of the transposition graph, meaning the optimal solution is exactly the minimum-cost Steiner Tree on the high-priority bays.
</PRESERVED_ANSWER>
<PRESERVED_CLASS>
The problem remains the Minimum Steiner Tree problem on an undirected graph, which is NP-complete.
</PRESERVED_CLASS>
<RISK>
A reader might misunderstand the target state as requiring all pairs to be swapped simultaneously rather than independently, but the text explicitly states "The specific sequence of activations may be different for each pair... transitions the warehouse from its initial state to a target state", making it clear each pair is a distinct scenario.
</RISK>
